**Most students don't have a study plan. They have a vague intention to "start soon" that turns into panic in the last ten days.** Then they re-read notes at 11pm, walk into the exam feeling like they've done something, and lose marks on questions they could have answered in September.

You have roughly eight weeks between now and NCEA externals in November. That is enough time to move most subjects up a grade, but only if the weeks are used differently from how most people use them. This is the plan. It's built on the two study techniques with the strongest evidence behind them, practice testing and spaced practice, which Dunlosky and colleagues rated as the highest-utility methods in their 2013 review. Everything else is scheduling.

## Before you start: know what you're actually aiming at

You can't plan a route without a destination. Spend 20 minutes on this before touching a textbook.

1. **List every external you're sitting.** Subject, standard, credits, and the grade you got in your last practice or mock.
2. **Work out what each one is worth to you.** If you already have enough credits to pass the level, some externals only matter for endorsement or for University Entrance. Others are the difference between passing and not. [Check where you stand on credits](/blog/ncea-credits-explained-how-many-to-pass) before you decide where the hours go.
3. **Rank them.** Top of the list: the externals where a grade jump is both valuable and realistic. Bottom: subjects you're already strong in, or ones where the grade barely changes anything.

That ranking decides how the eight weeks get split. A subject in the top third gets three or four sessions a week. A subject in the bottom third gets one. Do not spread yourself evenly. Even is how you end up with six Achieveds instead of three Merits.

## The four phases

The plan runs in four phases. The mistake most students make is spending all eight weeks in phase one.

| Phase | Weeks | What you're doing | Main tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Audit | 1 | Find out what you actually can't do | A full practice paper per subject, marked |
| 2. Rebuild | 2–4 | Retrieval practice on the weak topics | Blank-page recall, short practice questions |
| 3. Simulate | 5–6 | Full papers under exam conditions | Timed papers, honest marking |
| 4. Taper | 7–8 | Mocks, light review, sleep | One timed paper per subject, then rest |

### Week 1: audit

Do not open your notes. Sit one full practice paper for every external, closed book, timed. Mark it honestly. Write down every topic where you dropped marks and roughly how many.

This week feels bad. You'll get things wrong. That is the point. The paper is telling you exactly where the next seven weeks should go, and it's better to find out now than on exam day. If you want the audit done for you, the [free grade check](/grade) does exactly this: an 8-question paper, marked, with your weak topics listed.

By Sunday of week 1 you should have, for each subject, a list of three to six specific topics ranked by marks lost. Not "algebra". "Solving quadratics by factorising" or "interpreting the gradient in context."

### Weeks 2 to 4: rebuild with retrieval

This is where grades actually move. Take the weak-topic list and work through it using retrieval, not re-reading. The routine for each topic:

1. Read the relevant section of your notes once. Slowly. Once.
2. Close it. On a blank page, write everything you can remember, including a worked example.
3. Check what you missed. That gap is the learning.
4. Do three to five practice questions on that topic. Mark them. Write the correction next to each mistake.
5. Two days later, do two more questions on the same topic without looking at anything first.

Step five is what makes it stick. [Spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide) works because the second retrieval, after you've started to forget, is a harder and more valuable one. [Roediger and Karpicke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testing_effect)'s testing-effect research showed retrieval practice beats extra study time on the same material, and the gap widens the longer the delay before the test. November is a long delay. Use it.

Rotate topics. Don't spend a whole session on one thing. Two topics per session, alternated, is harder and produces better recall than blocking.

<Callout type="warning">
If you find yourself highlighting, copying out notes, or watching a video "to refresh", stop. Those feel like study and produce almost nothing you can retrieve under pressure. The only question that matters is: can you produce it on a blank page? If not, you haven't learned it yet.
</Callout>

### Weeks 5 to 6: simulate

Now switch from topics to whole papers. Twice a week per priority subject, sit a full paper under exam conditions: timed, closed book, in one sitting, no phone. Then mark it properly and write a one-line note on every lost mark.

Two things happen here. First, you find the gaps that only show up under time pressure, like running out of time on the last question or misreading the command word. Second, you build the stamina to concentrate for the full exam length, which is a skill you can't get from 20-minute sessions.

Use the free past papers and exemplars NZQA publishes on the [NCEA site](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/) for at least one of your weekly papers so you're calibrated to the real format. Use generated NCEA-style papers for the rest, so you never run out of fresh questions on your weak topics. Our guide to [using past papers properly](/blog/how-to-use-past-papers-mock-exam-strategy) covers how to get the most out of each one.

Go back to phase-two retrieval for any topic that keeps costing marks. Don't just sit more papers and hope.

### Weeks 7 to 8: taper

The last fortnight is not for learning new content. It's for consolidating, calibrating, and arriving at the exam rested.

- One timed paper per subject in week 7. Mark it. Fix the two or three things it exposes.
- Week 8: short daily retrieval sessions, 20 to 30 minutes, cycling through your weak-topic list. No new material.
- Sleep. Seven to nine hours, every night, all week. Memory consolidation happens while you sleep, and an all-nighter costs you more marks than it gains.
- The day before each exam: a light review of your correction notes. Then stop.

If exam nerves are a problem for you, this is also the fortnight to practise the routines in [how to beat test anxiety before exams](/blog/how-to-beat-test-anxiety-before-exams). Rehearse them during your timed papers so they're automatic on the day.

## A sample week (phase 2)

Here's what a phase-two week looks like for a student sitting four externals, with Maths and Science as priorities, English mid-priority, and Geography already strong.

| Day | Session 1 (45 min) | Session 2 (45 min) |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Maths: weak topic A, retrieval + 4 questions | Science: weak topic A, retrieval + 4 questions |
| Tuesday | English: unfamiliar text, one timed response | Maths: weak topic B |
| Wednesday | Science: weak topic B | Geography: 4 questions on weakest topic |
| Thursday | Maths: topics A + B, 2 questions each, no notes first | English: essay plan + one paragraph, marked |
| Friday | Science: topics A + B, 2 questions each, no notes first | Rest |
| Saturday | Maths: new weak topic C | Science: new weak topic C |
| Sunday | Review all corrections from the week, 30 min | Rest |

Nine sessions, under seven hours total. The point isn't volume. It's that every session ends with you retrieving something, getting it marked, and seeing what you got wrong.

Adjust the split to your own ranking. If you're not sure how to fit this around school and work, [how to make a study timetable you'll actually follow](/blog/how-to-make-a-study-timetable-you-will-follow) walks through building one that survives contact with real life.

## The rules that make the plan work

**Mark everything, honestly.** A practice question you don't mark is a practice question you didn't do. Marking yourself leniently is worse than not marking at all, because it tells you a topic is safe when it isn't.

**Track marks lost, not hours spent.** Keep a single page per subject: topic, marks lost, date. When a topic's line stops growing, it's fixed. Hours are a vanity metric.

**Retrieval first, notes second.** Open your notes to check, never to start. If you can't do it from a blank page, you can't do it in the exam.

**Never spend a session without a question.** Reading, watching, summarising: none of it counts as a session unless you finished it by answering something and checking the answer.

**Rest is part of the plan.** Two rest sessions a week are scheduled, not optional. Burnout in week 5 costs you more than the hours it saves.

## What if you're starting late?

If you're reading this with four weeks to go instead of eight, compress rather than skip. One week of audit becomes two days. Rebuild becomes two weeks, on your top two subjects only. Simulate becomes one week. Taper stays at five days. The order stays the same because the order is the point: you can't fix what you haven't found, and you can't perform what you haven't rehearsed.

If you have less than two weeks, the plan is different and it's covered in [how to cram for an exam the night before](/blog/how-to-cram-for-an-exam-the-night-before). Even then, retrieval beats re-reading.

## Questions students ask about this plan

**How many hours a day?** In phases 2 and 3, one to two 45-minute sessions on school days and two or three on weekends is plenty for most students. If you're doing more than three hours a day and not improving, the problem is method, not time.

**Should I study every subject every week?** No. Priority subjects get three or four sessions. Strong subjects get one. A subject that changes nothing for you (already passed, no endorsement in reach) can get none until the taper.

**What if my school's mocks are in the middle of this?** Treat them as a free phase-3 paper. Sit them properly, get the marked paper back, and feed every lost mark into your weak-topic list. Then carry on with the plan.

**Is it too late if I've missed most of the year?** If you can still pass the level, no. Compress the plan as described above and focus on the externals with the most credits where you're closest to Achieved.

## Start today, not Monday

The single most common failure is waiting for a clean start. There is no clean start. Sit one practice paper today, in whatever subject is worrying you most, and mark it. That's week 1 begun.
