**Most students can't tell you, off the top of their head, how many credits they have or how many they need.** They know it's "60-something" and that some credits "carry over", and then in October they discover they've been stressing about a subject that doesn't change anything, while ignoring one that does.

This is the whole system in one page. Read it, then go and count your own credits. It takes ten minutes and it changes how you should spend the next eight weeks.

## The short version

| Certificate | Credits needed | At what level | Extra requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| NCEA Level 1 | 60 | Level 1 or above | 20-credit literacy and numeracy co-requisite |
| NCEA Level 2 | 60 | Level 2 or above | Co-requisite (usually already met at Level 1) |
| NCEA Level 3 | 60 | Level 3 or above | Co-requisite (usually already met) |

Three numbers to remember: 60, 60, 60. Each level needs 60 credits earned at that level or higher. A Level 2 credit counts towards Level 1, and a Level 3 credit counts towards Level 1 and Level 2. A Level 1 credit never counts towards Level 2 or 3.

Everything else is detail. Important detail, but detail.

## What a credit actually is

Every NCEA subject is broken into standards. Each standard is worth a set number of credits, usually somewhere between 3 and 6. When you achieve the standard, at Achieved, Merit or Excellence, you get all of its credits. If you get Not Achieved, you get none of them. There are no partial credits for a standard.

That has a consequence a lot of students miss: a 4-credit standard at Achieved and a 4-credit standard at Excellence are worth exactly the same number of credits towards passing. The grade matters for [endorsement](/blog/ncea-endorsement-merit-excellence) and for how the work feels, but for the pass-or-fail question, credits are credits.

Standards come in two kinds:

- **Internals** are assessed at school during the year. Most of these are already done by September.
- **Externals** are the exams in November. These are the credits still in play.

## The literacy and numeracy co-requisite

Since 2024, passing any NCEA level also requires a separate 20-credit literacy and numeracy co-requisite: 10 credits for literacy (split into reading and writing) and 10 for numeracy. These are usually earned through the Common Assessment Activities, the CAAs, which most students sit in Year 10 or 11.

Two things to know:

1. The co-requisite credits sit outside the 60. They don't count towards the 60 for your certificate, and the 60 don't count towards the co-requisite.
2. Once you've met it, it's met. You don't re-do it at Level 2 or Level 3.

There have been transition arrangements while the new system beds in, and the rules for who has to meet the co-requisite which way have changed more than once. Check the current position on [NZQA's NCEA pages](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/) rather than trusting a friend's memory of what applied last year.

<Callout type="info">
If you haven't met the co-requisite yet, it is your highest priority. It doesn't matter how many subject credits you collect; without the co-requisite you don't get the certificate. Ask your dean today where you stand.
</Callout>

## Level 1: 60 credits at Level 1 or above

The most common Year 11 situation is five or six subjects, each offering roughly 15 to 20 credits over the year. That's around 90 to 110 credits on offer. You need 60 of them plus the co-requisite.

So you can, in theory, fail a whole subject and still pass Level 1. Plenty of students do. The pass mark is not the problem. The problem is that "just passing" Level 1 with no Merits or Excellences tells universities and employers very little, and it means no endorsement.

## Level 2: 60 credits at Level 2 or above

Same number, one level up. Level 1 credits from last year don't count here; you're starting the count again with this year's Level 2 (and any Level 3) credits.

Level 2 is the certificate many students are actually judged on, because it's the last full year of results available when university and scholarship applications go in. It's also where course endorsements start to matter for UE-approved subjects. If you're in Year 12, the externals in November are worth more attention than they got in Year 11.

## Level 3: 60 credits at Level 3 or above

Same again, but with a second question sitting behind it: are you also getting University Entrance?

UE is a separate award. It requires Level 3, plus 14 credits at Level 3 in each of three approved subjects, plus UE literacy (10 credits at Level 2 or above, 5 reading and 5 writing) and numeracy (10 credits at Level 1 or above). So you can have 60 Level 3 credits and still miss UE if they're spread thinly across five subjects instead of stacked to 14 in three approved ones.

If you're in Year 13 and university is the plan, count your approved-subject credits separately from your total. The full breakdown is in [University Entrance requirements explained](/blog/university-entrance-nz-requirements-explained).

## How to count your own credits (do this now)

1. Log in to your NZQA learner account, or ask your dean for a credit summary. Internals your school has already reported will show there.
2. Add internals you've completed that haven't been reported yet. Your teachers will know.
3. Add up what you have at the level you're sitting.
4. Subtract from 60. That's your gap.
5. List the externals you're sitting, with their credit values. That's what's still available.

Now you know two numbers: how many credits you still need, and how many are on the table in November. The ratio between them tells you how much pressure you're actually under, which is often less than it feels, and occasionally more.

## What this means for how you study

This is the part that matters. The credit rules should change where your hours go.

**If you've already passed the level on internals**, the externals are about grades, not credits. Your question becomes: which exams can I realistically move from Achieved to Merit, or Merit to Excellence? Put your time into those. Two subjects moved up a grade beats five subjects held steady.

**If you're short of 60**, prioritise the externals with the most credits where you're closest to Achieved. A 5-credit standard you're nearly passing is worth more of your time than a 4-credit standard you're already comfortable in, or a 6-credit one you're nowhere near.

**Don't chase credits you don't need.** If you're at 58 with three externals worth 15 credits between them, one Achieved gets you there. Spend the rest of the time on the exam that matters for endorsement or UE.

**Externals are the only credits you can still change.** Internals are done. Whatever the gap is, it closes in November or it doesn't. That makes the next eight weeks disproportionately valuable, and it's why the [8-week NCEA study plan](/blog/how-to-study-for-ncea-exams) starts with sitting a practice paper in every external to find out where you stand.

## Common questions

**Do credits carry over between years?** Higher-level credits count down. Level 2 credits count towards Level 1; Level 3 credits count towards Level 1 and Level 2. Lower-level credits never count up.

**Can I pass Level 2 without passing Level 1?** You need 60 credits at Level 2 or above to get Level 2. In practice, if you have those you'll usually also meet Level 1, since Level 2 credits count towards it. The co-requisite still applies.

**What happens if I fail an external?** You get zero credits for that standard. Your internals are unaffected. If you still have 60 at the level from everything else, you still pass.

**Does Not Achieved show on my record?** Yes. It doesn't remove credits, but it appears on your Record of Achievement.

**Where's the official version of all this?** [NZQA](https://www.nzqa.govt.nz/) is the source of truth. If anything here disagrees with NZQA, NZQA is right.

## The point

Sixty credits per level, at that level or above, plus the co-requisite. Count what you have. Work out what's still in play. Then study the externals that actually change your outcome, and don't waste September on the ones that don't.
