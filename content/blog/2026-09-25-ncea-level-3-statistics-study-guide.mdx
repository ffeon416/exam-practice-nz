**Most students lose marks in Level 3 Statistics on questions they calculated correctly.** The number was right. The working was right. Then they wrote "0.914" on the answer line, moved on, and left the Merit and Excellence marks sitting on the page.

Level 3 Statistics is not a calculation subject. The calculator does the calculating. The exam is testing whether you can choose the right tool, justify the choice, and say what the number means for the situation in the question. If you can do that, the maths you already know is enough. If you can't, a perfect calculation earns you Achieved at best.

This guide covers what the three externals actually test, the difference between a calculation and a justified conclusion, the five places marks disappear, a 4-week routine, and two worked examples showing a weak answer, what the examiner wanted, and the fix.

## What the Level 3 statistics externals actually test

Which externals you sit depends on what your school has entered you for, so check with your teacher and the timetable on [NZQA's NCEA site](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/). The three commonly sat are:

- **Probability concepts.** Probabilities from tables, tree diagrams and Venn diagrams. Conditional probability, independence, mutually exclusive events, relative risk, and explaining what "the probability is 0.3 given that..." means in context. The Excellence questions ask you to compare or interpret, not just compute.
- **Probability distributions.** Choosing the right model (binomial, Poisson, normal, and sometimes uniform or triangular), justifying the choice against the conditions of the situation, calculating a probability or an expected value, and commenting on whether the model is reasonable. Much of the credit is for the justification and the comment.
- **Evaluating statistically based reports.** Reading a report (a news article, a survey summary, a study) and assessing its claims. Sampling method, sample size, margin of error, confidence intervals, non-sampling errors, observational study versus experiment, and whether the conclusion is supported by the evidence. This paper is almost entirely written reasoning.

The common thread: every question ends in context. A probability is about a real thing. A margin of error is about a real claim. The marker is looking for a sentence that connects the number to the situation.

## A calculation is not a conclusion

This is the distinction that separates Achieved from Merit and Excellence across all three papers.

| What you wrote | What it is | Grade it tends to reach |
|---|---|---|
| "P(X ≤ 1) = 0.914" | A calculation | Achieved |
| "The probability that at most one component in the sample is faulty is 0.914." | A calculation in context | Achieved, sometimes Merit |
| "The probability is 0.914, so about nine in ten samples of this size would have at most one faulty component. This is high enough that finding two or more faulty components would be surprising if the company's claim is true." | A justified conclusion | Merit or Excellence, depending on the question |

The third row does three things the first row doesn't. It says what the number refers to. It says what it means in ordinary language. It uses it to answer the question that was actually asked. That's the pattern for nearly every Merit and Excellence answer in statistics: **number, meaning, so what.** It's the statistics version of the grade ladder described in [what examiners look for at each grade](/blog/achieved-merit-excellence-what-examiners-look-for).

## Where the marks are lost

These are the ones that come up again and again in marked practice papers. None of them are about not knowing the statistics.

| Mistake | What it looks like | Why it costs marks |
|---|---|---|
| **Quoting without interpreting** | "The margin of error is about 3 percentage points." Full stop. | The mark is for what that means: the claim in the report is or isn't supported once you allow for it |
| **No justification for the model** | Straight to the binomial calculation with no mention of why binomial | Choosing the distribution is often worth as much as the calculation. Unjustified means unmarked |
| **Generic criticism** | "The sample size is too small" for a sample of a thousand | Markers recognise a memorised objection that doesn't fit. Criticism has to be specific to the report in front of you |
| **Confusing correlation with causation, or the reverse** | "The study proves exercise causes better mental health" from a survey | Observational data can show an association. Only a well-designed experiment supports a causal claim |
| **Ignoring the model's assumptions** | Using a Poisson model without checking the events are independent and at a constant average rate | The Excellence comment is usually "is this model reasonable, and why might it not be?" Skipping the assumptions skips that mark |

## The 4-week routine

This assumes four weeks and roughly four sessions a week for statistics. If you're balancing several externals, [the 8-week NCEA plan](/blog/how-to-study-for-ncea-exams) shows how to weight them.

The principle for all four weeks: **you learn statistics by writing justified conclusions and getting them marked, not by watching worked examples.** Practice testing is one of the two highest-utility study methods in Dunlosky and colleagues' 2013 review, and the reasons are covered in [why practice beats re-reading](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading).

### Week 1: audit

- Sit one practice paper per external, timed, closed book, with your calculator. Don't study first.
- Mark it honestly against a marking schedule. For each question, note whether you lost marks on the calculation, the justification, or the interpretation.
- List the topics where you got stuck, and separately list the questions where you calculated correctly but didn't write a conclusion. The second list is usually longer, and faster to fix.

### Week 2: rebuild the reasoning

- One topic per session. Read your notes once. Close them.
- On a blank page, write out the reasoning template for that topic. For distributions: the conditions for each model, and the one-line justification for each condition. For reports: the list of things to check, and what each one means for a claim.
- Do three or four questions, and for every one, write the full "number, meaning, so what" sentence even if the question only asked for the number.
- Two days later, redo two of them from memory. [Spaced retrieval](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide) is what makes the reasoning stick.

### Week 3: practise the writing

- Take each question from Week 1 where you calculated correctly but lost marks. Rewrite the answer with a full justification and conclusion. Compare against the schedule.
- For the reports paper, practise the discipline of reading the whole report before writing anything. Underline the claim. Then ask: what evidence is offered, and does it support that claim?
- Start every session with two reasoning templates from Week 2, from memory.

### Week 4: rehearse the exam

- Two full timed papers, one at the start of the week and one at the end. Mark both. [How to use past papers properly](/blog/how-to-use-past-papers-mock-exam-strategy) covers the method.
- Between them, rewrite every answer that stopped at the calculation.
- Last two days: no new content. Redo your five weakest questions from a blank page, full conclusions included.

<Callout type="warning">
Don't spend Week 4 re-reading formula sheets. You'll have the formulae in the exam. What you won't have is the habit of turning each number into a sentence, and that habit only comes from writing the sentences.
</Callout>

## Worked example 1: choosing and justifying a distribution

**Question.** A factory claims that the probability any given component is faulty is 0.05. A quality inspector takes a random sample of 10 components from a large batch.

(a) State a suitable distribution for the number of faulty components in the sample and justify your choice.
(b) Calculate the probability that at most one component in the sample is faulty.
(c) The inspector finds 3 faulty components in the sample. Comment on the factory's claim.

**Weak answer (Achieved at best):**

> (a) Binomial.
> (b) P(X ≤ 1) = 0.914
> (c) That's unlikely so the claim is wrong.

The distribution is right. The probability is right. But (a) has no justification, (b) has no working and no context, and (c) is an opinion with no number behind it.

**What the examiner wanted:** in (a), the conditions of a binomial situation checked against this scenario; in (b), the working and the answer in context; in (c), a calculation of how unlikely the observation is if the claim is true, followed by a measured conclusion that recognises the limits of a single sample.

**The fix:**

> (a) Let X be the number of faulty components in the sample. X is binomial with n = 10 and p = 0.05, because: there is a fixed number of trials (10 components are inspected); each trial has two outcomes (faulty or not faulty); the probability of a fault is the same for every component (0.05, as claimed); and the trials are independent, because the batch is large, so removing a component barely changes the probability for the next one.
>
> (b) P(X ≤ 1) = P(X = 0) + P(X = 1)
> P(X = 0) = 0.95^10 = 0.5987
> P(X = 1) = 10 × 0.05 × 0.95^9 = 0.3151
> P(X ≤ 1) = 0.5987 + 0.3151 = 0.9139
> So the probability that at most one component in the sample is faulty is about 0.914.
>
> (c) If the claim is true, the probability of finding 3 or more faulty components is
> P(X ≥ 3) = 1 − P(X ≤ 2)
> P(X = 2) = 45 × 0.05² × 0.95^8 = 0.0746
> P(X ≤ 2) = 0.9139 + 0.0746 = 0.9885
> P(X ≥ 3) = 1 − 0.9885 = 0.0115
> A result this extreme would happen in only about 1 in 87 samples if the fault rate really were 0.05. That is strong evidence the true fault rate is higher than the factory claims. However, this is one sample of 10 components. Rare results do happen, and the inspector should take further samples before concluding the claim is false. The binomial model also assumes faults are independent; if faulty components tend to occur in clusters (for example from one bad production run), the model would understate the probability of seeing several in one sample.

Part (c) calculates the right probability, turns it into plain language ("about 1 in 87 samples"), draws a conclusion, then qualifies it by sample size and by the model's assumptions. That final qualification is where the Excellence mark usually lives.

## Worked example 2: evaluating a claim in a report

**Question.** A news article reports: "A survey of 1,000 New Zealand adults found that 62% of those who exercise at least three times a week rated their mental health as good, compared with 48% of those who exercise less often. The results show that regular exercise improves mental health." The article says the survey has a margin of error of about 3 percentage points. Evaluate the claim that regular exercise improves mental health.

**Weak answer (Achieved at best):**

> The sample size of 1,000 is too small to represent all of New Zealand. Also, the survey doesn't say how people were chosen so it could be biased. The claim is not valid.

The second sentence is a fair point. The first is wrong: a well-chosen sample of 1,000 is perfectly usable for a population-level estimate. And the answer never mentions the biggest problem with the claim.

**What the examiner wanted:** the distinction between an observational study and an experiment; alternative explanations for the difference; a comment on the margin of error as it applies to the comparison being made; and a conclusion about what the data can and cannot support.

**The fix:**

> The claim is about causation: that exercise *improves* mental health. But the data comes from a survey, which is an observational study. People chose for themselves whether to exercise, so the two groups may differ in many other ways. For example, people who already have better mental health may find it easier to exercise regularly, so the direction of the effect could be the reverse of what the article claims. Other factors such as income, age or physical health could also explain both the exercise habit and the mental health rating. Only a randomised experiment, where participants are assigned to exercise or not, could support a causal claim.
>
> The survey does show an association: a difference of 14 percentage points between the two groups. The margin of error of about 3 percentage points applies to a proportion calculated from the whole sample of 1,000. Each exercise group is smaller than that, so the margin of error for each group's proportion is larger, and the margin of error for the difference between the two groups is larger again. The difference of 14 percentage points is still well outside the likely error, so it is reasonable to conclude that a real association exists in the population, provided the sample was selected in a way that represents New Zealand adults.
>
> Two further limitations: the mental health rating is self-reported, so how people interpret "good" may differ; and the article does not say how the 1,000 adults were selected. If the survey was self-selected (for example an online poll), the results may not represent the population at all.
>
> Conclusion: the data supports a claim that people who exercise regularly are *more likely to rate* their mental health as good. It does not support the claim that exercise *improves* mental health. The article's conclusion goes further than the evidence allows.

Notice the structure: type of claim, type of study, specific alternative explanations, the margin of error handled properly, then a conclusion that says exactly what the data does and doesn't show. Nothing generic, and no recycled objections that don't fit.

## Questions students ask

**How much working do I need to show for a calculator answer?** Enough that a marker can see which distribution, which parameters, and which probability you asked the calculator for. "X ~ B(10, 0.05), P(X ≤ 1) = 0.914" is enough for the calculation mark. It's not enough for the question, which almost always wants a sentence after it.

**The reports paper has so many things I could criticise. How do I choose?** Pick the ones that actually affect the claim. Ask "if this problem is real, does the conclusion still stand?" Lead with the problems where the answer is no. Three well-chosen, well-explained points beat eight vague ones.

**What if I'm not sure which distribution to use?** Write the conditions for the one you think fits and check them against the question. If a condition clearly fails, say so; the examiner often wants exactly that comment. And never leave the question blank. A justified choice with a small slip in the calculation beats an empty page, as [how to cram for an exam the night before](/blog/how-to-cram-for-an-exam-the-night-before) points out.

## What to do today

Take any probability question you've done this term. Cover your answer. Redo it, and this time write three lines after the number: what it refers to, what it means in plain language, and what it says about the question that was asked. Then check whether your original answer had any of those three.

That's ten minutes, and it's the single change that moves most statistics students up a grade.
