**Most students look at the NCEA exam timetable once, note their first paper, and then study as if every exam was on the same day.** They spend the week before their first external revising all five subjects at once, walk into paper one under-prepared, and only then realise the second paper is tomorrow.

The timetable is not just a list of dates. It's the shape of your November. Once you know the gap before each paper, you know exactly how much time each subject can get and when. That turns a month of vague worry into a series of short, specific jobs.

This post shows you how to read the timetable properly, size each gap, decide which subjects get the hours, and what to do on the awkward days: the day before, the day after, and the ones where two papers land back to back.

## Step 1: get the real timetable, not a rumour

NZQA publishes the external exam timetable every year on the [NCEA site](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/). Your school will also give you a personalised version showing only the papers you're entered for. Use that one, and check it against the NZQA version. Don't rely on what a friend remembers.

Once you have it, write every paper you're sitting onto one calendar page, in order, with the date and the session (morning or afternoon). Add your school's last day of classes and any study leave. That single page is what everything else in this post builds on.

<Callout type="warning">
Check the session as well as the date. A morning paper on Tuesday means Monday night is your last real preparation. An afternoon paper on Tuesday gives you Tuesday morning too. Those are different plans.
</Callout>

## Step 2: work out the gap before each paper

For every paper, count the clear days between it and the previous one. The first paper's gap runs from the end of classes (or the start of study leave) to the exam.

You'll usually find three kinds of gap:

- **A long gap** (roughly five days or more). Enough to fix real weaknesses, not just revise.
- **A short gap** (two to four days). Enough for one full timed paper and targeted corrections, not enough to learn new content.
- **No gap** (consecutive days, or two papers on one day). All the real preparation for the second paper has to happen before the first.

Write the gap next to each paper. Most students are surprised by how uneven it is. You might have nine days before one exam and one day before the next. The uneven shape is the whole reason to plan.

## Step 3: allocate each gap by marks at stake and current grade

A gap is a budget. The question is how to spend it, and the answer is not "evenly across every subject still to come."

Two things decide how much of a gap a subject gets:

1. **What's at stake.** How many credits is the paper worth, and what does the grade change? If you need this paper to pass the level, or it decides an endorsement, or it's a University Entrance subject, it ranks high. If you've already got the credits you need and the grade changes nothing, it ranks low. [NCEA credits explained](/blog/ncea-credits-explained-how-many-to-pass) walks through how to work this out.
2. **How far you are from the next grade.** A subject sitting just below Merit is worth more hours than one comfortably at Excellence or one stuck well below Achieved. Move the grades that are movable.

Combine the two and you get a priority order. Then, inside each gap, the paper coming next gets most of the time, but the paper after it gets some too, especially if its own gap is short.

A rough rule that works: in any gap, about two thirds of your sessions go to the next paper, and the rest go to the paper after it. If the paper after it has no gap of its own, shift the split until you've done at least one full timed paper for it before the first exam.

<Callout type="info">
Sessions, not hours. Plan in 45-minute blocks with a specific task each ("Chemistry: one timed practice paper, marked", "English: one essay paragraph to an unseen question"). A block with a task gets done. "Study Chemistry, 3pm to 6pm" gets postponed.
</Callout>

## Step 4: the routines for the days that matter most

### The day before a paper

The day before is not for learning. It's for calibrating and settling.

- **Morning:** one short retrieval session on the subject, from a blank page. Weakest topics first. No new content.
- **Afternoon:** read through your corrections page for that subject, the list of every mistake you've made in practice and what the fix was. This is the highest-value reading you'll do all month, because every line on it is a mark you've already lost once.
- **Evening:** pack your gear (ID, pens, calculator if allowed, water), confirm the time and room, then stop. Nothing after 9pm. Sleep does more for tomorrow's recall than another hour of notes. If nerves are the problem rather than knowledge, the night-before routine in [how to beat test anxiety](/blog/how-to-beat-test-anxiety-before-exams) is built for exactly this.

If you're reading this with one night and a mountain of gaps, the honest triage version is in [how to cram the night before](/blog/how-to-cram-for-an-exam-the-night-before). It works better than you'd expect, and worse than a plan.

### The day after a paper

Two rules.

**Don't post-mortem the paper.** Ten minutes to note anything you'd do differently in the exam room (ran out of time, misread a command word), then close it. You can't change the mark now, and replaying it steals hours from the next one.

**Restart on the next subject the same day.** Not a full session if you're tired, but at least one retrieval block. The next paper's gap started the moment you put your pen down. The habit of "I earned a day off" is how a four-day gap becomes a two-day gap.

### When two papers are on consecutive days

This is the situation students handle worst, and it's entirely predictable from the timetable.

The preparation for the second paper has to be finished before the first. Treat the two as one unit with a combined gap. Sit a full timed paper for the second subject at least two days before the first exam, so you know its weak topics. In the last two days before the first exam, split your sessions between the two, with the first paper getting the day-before routine above.

Then on the evening between the two papers: corrections page for paper two, gear packed, bed. That's it. You do not learn anything new on that evening. Everything you can retrieve tomorrow was learned before today.

If two papers fall on the same day (morning and afternoon), the same logic applies with even less room. Prepare both fully in advance, and use the break between them to eat, walk, and read one corrections page. Nothing else.

### When there's a long gap

A gap of a week or more is a gift with a trap inside it. The gift is time to fix things properly. The trap is losing sharpness on the subject you'll sit at the end of it, and losing the exam rhythm you built in week one.

Use a long gap like a compressed version of the [8-week NCEA plan](/blog/how-to-study-for-ncea-exams): one timed paper early to find the gaps, [retrieval practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testing_effect) on the weak topics through the middle, a second timed paper two or three days out, then taper. Keep one short retrieval session going for any later subject too, so it doesn't go cold.

And keep sitting timed papers. The skill of concentrating for the full length of an exam fades quickly. If your last full paper was ten days ago, your first hour back in the exam room will feel slow.

## A worked example (invented, to show the method)

Here's a made-up timetable for a student, call her Tessa, sitting five externals. The subjects and dates are placeholders. Your own timetable will look different, and you must use the real one from NZQA.

| Paper | Day | Session | Gap before it | Stake | Current grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| English | Week 1, Monday | Morning | 6 days (from end of classes) | UE subject, endorsement in reach | Merit |
| Biology | Week 1, Thursday | Afternoon | 2 days | Needs credits to pass level | Just under Achieved |
| Maths | Week 1, Friday | Morning | 0 days (consecutive) | Already has credits; endorsement possible | Merit |
| Chemistry | Week 2, Wednesday | Morning | 4 days | Needed for a university course | Achieved |
| Geography | Week 3, Thursday | Afternoon | 7 days | Strong, changes nothing | Excellence |

Tessa's priority order, combining stake and distance to the next grade: Biology (passing depends on it and it's close), Chemistry (matters for next year, a grade jump is realistic), English (endorsement in reach), Maths (nice to have), Geography (already there).

Her plan by gap:

- **Six days before English.** English gets about two thirds of sessions. But Biology and Maths are stacked right behind it with almost no room, so the other third goes to a full timed Biology paper and a timed Maths paper during this gap, both marked, weak topics listed. She now knows what to fix for both before she's sat a single exam.
- **Two days before Biology, zero before Maths.** Treat as one unit. Day after English: one Biology retrieval block that afternoon, then Biology corrections. Next day: Biology weak topics in the morning, Maths corrections page and two questions per weak topic in the afternoon. Evening before Biology: the day-before routine for Biology. Evening between Biology and Maths: Maths corrections page, gear, bed.
- **Four days before Chemistry.** A short gap, so no new content. Day after Maths: rest until afternoon, then one timed Chemistry paper. Two days of retrieval on what it exposed. Day before: routine.
- **Seven days before Geography.** She's already at Excellence and it changes nothing, so this is her lightest week. One timed paper early, one short session most days on the topics it exposes, one more timed paper three days out. Rest properly. The risk here is going flat, not going in unprepared.

Notice what the plan does. Biology, the subject she most needs, gets serious work before her first exam even though it's her second paper. That only happens because she read the timetable and saw the two-day gap coming.

## The rules that make the plan work

**Mark every timed paper honestly, the same day.** An unmarked paper tells you nothing about which gap to spend where. [How to use past papers properly](/blog/how-to-use-past-papers-mock-exam-strategy) covers the marking method.

**Keep a corrections page per subject.** One line per mistake: what went wrong, what the fix is. It becomes the only thing you read the day before each paper.

**Prepare the paper behind the paper.** Whenever a gap is short, the work for that paper moves earlier. Never assume you'll "have the evening."

**Rest is scheduled.** An exam is a full-day effort. The afternoon after a morning paper is rest unless another paper is tomorrow.

**No new content once exams start.** From your first paper onward, you're retrieving and correcting. If a topic is still unlearned on the first day of exams, it's a decision about whether to drop it, not a reason to start it.

## Questions students ask

**What if my school's study leave starts before my first exam?** That's your first gap, and it's usually the longest one you'll get. Use it to sit a timed paper for every subject, not just the first, so you know the weak topics for the whole month.

**Should I study for a later paper on the day of an earlier one?** Only if the later paper is tomorrow. Otherwise, sit the exam, rest, and start the next subject the following morning. You'll get more from a fresh session than a tired one.

**My worst subject has the shortest gap. What do I do?** Move its preparation earlier. The gap on the timetable is just the gap between exams. Your preparation gap can start whenever you decide it does.

**How do I stop a long gap from going to waste?** Book the timed papers in first, one early and one late, and put them on the calendar like exams. The days between them fill themselves.
