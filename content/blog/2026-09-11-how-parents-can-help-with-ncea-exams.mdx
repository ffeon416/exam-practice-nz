**Most parents want to help with exams and end up helping with stress instead.** Not because they're doing anything unreasonable. Because "have you studied?" asked at 9pm on a Tuesday lands as pressure, not support, and because the things that actually move a grade are mostly invisible from the kitchen.

This is a guide for parents of students sitting NCEA. It covers what the system is, what helps, what hurts, how to tell whether the studying happening in the bedroom is real, and what to do about the cost of getting help.

## NCEA in 200 words

NCEA is New Zealand's secondary school qualification. It has three levels, usually done in Years 11, 12 and 13. Each level needs 60 credits at that level or above. Credits come from standards, which are individual assessed pieces of work in each subject.

Some standards are **internal**: assessed by the teacher during the year, so those results are mostly locked in by term three. Some are **external**: sat as exams in November and marked by NZQA. For most academic subjects, the externals are a large share of the credits, which is why November matters.

Each standard is graded Not Achieved, Achieved, Merit or Excellence. A student passes the level with 60 credits at any passing grade. They earn a [Merit or Excellence endorsement](/blog/ncea-endorsement-merit-excellence) on the certificate with 50 credits at that grade or higher.

There is also a 20-credit literacy and numeracy co-requisite for the qualification, introduced in 2024. Most students meet it in Year 10 or 11.

For anything specific to your child's subjects or year, the source is [NZQA's NCEA site](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/). If you want more on how credits add up, [how many credits you need to pass NCEA](/blog/ncea-credits-explained-how-many-to-pass) is written for students but works for parents too.

## What actually helps

The things that help are unglamorous. They're about the conditions around the study, not the study itself.

**Structure.** Teenagers are bad at starting and good at continuing. A fixed study slot at the same time every day, agreed in advance and short enough to be non-negotiable (forty-five minutes, say), removes the daily argument about when. If they want more, great. The slot is the floor.

**A quiet space.** Not necessarily a desk in a bedroom. Some students work better at the kitchen table with a parent nearby. Ask which they prefer and then protect it: no siblings, no TV, no "can you just quickly."

**Food and sleep.** This sounds like a cliché until you watch a sixteen-year-old try to do calculus on four hours' sleep and a packet of chips. Regular meals and a consistent bedtime do more for exam performance than any study technique. The week before an exam is not the week to let the routine slide.

**Asking the right question.** "Did you study?" invites "yes" and ends the conversation. Try "what did you practise today?" It's specific, it assumes they did something, and the answer tells you a lot. "I did three past-paper questions on genetics and got two right" is real study. "I read over my notes" is a warning sign, for reasons covered below.

**Logistics.** Knowing the exam timetable, having the right calculator, getting to the venue with time to spare. Your child's school will publish the timetable and NZQA has the official one. Being the person who quietly handles this is enormously helpful and completely invisible.

**Being calm on the day.** Whatever you feel about the exam, they need you to be the steady one. A relaxed breakfast and "you've done the work, go and show them" beats last-minute quizzing every time.

<Callout type="tip">
The single most useful sentence a parent can say in exam season: "What can I take off your plate this week?" Chores, driving, a younger sibling. Removing friction from the rest of their life is help they will actually accept.
</Callout>

## What quietly makes it worse

**Nagging.** Every reminder after the first one is subtracted from their motivation, not added to it. If the study slot is agreed, trust the slot. If it's being skipped, that's a conversation about the plan, not a nightly reminder.

**Comparing.** With siblings, with cousins, with your own school days, with the kid down the road who got Excellence endorsed. It never motivates. It only tells them you're keeping score.

**Surprise tutoring.** Booking a tutor without discussing it reads as "I don't think you can do this." If tutoring is on the table, raise it as an option they get a say in, and be specific about what it's for: one subject, one topic, a set number of sessions.

**Treating every exam as make-or-break.** NCEA is credit-based. A rough result in one external is rarely the end of anything. Students who believe everything rides on one paper freeze in that paper. If your child is prone to this, [how to beat test anxiety before exams](/blog/how-to-beat-test-anxiety-before-exams) is written for them and worth sharing.

**Making study visible for your benefit.** Some parents feel reassured by a teenager sitting at a desk with books open. The teenager learns to perform studying. Hours at the desk is not the measure. Questions answered correctly is.

## How to tell if the study is real

This is the part most parents never get told, and it's the part that matters most.

The research on how people learn is consistent: re-reading notes and highlighting feel productive and do very little. Testing yourself, from memory, and then checking the answer, is what builds knowledge that survives an exam. Roediger and Karpicke's work on what's called the testing effect showed this clearly, and later reviews of study techniques have rated practice testing and spaced practice as the most effective methods available. The full explanation is in [why practice exams beat re-reading](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading).

What this means for you: the question isn't whether your child is studying. It's whether they are **practising** or **reviewing**.

Signs of real practice:

- They can show you a question they attempted and how it was marked.
- They can tell you a topic they got wrong this week and what they've done about it.
- They're doing questions under some kind of time limit.
- Their notes are getting *used* (covered up, tested against), not just *made*.

Signs of reviewing dressed up as studying:

- Beautiful colour-coded notes and no answered questions.
- "I went over the whole topic" but they can't do a question on it from scratch.
- Highlighters. Lots of highlighters.
- Confidence that drops sharply the moment the book is closed.

You don't need to understand the subject to check this. Ask to see a marked practice question. If there aren't any, that's the thing to fix, and it's usually a small fix: more questions, fewer notes.

<Callout type="warning">
A teenager who studies four hours a night by re-reading can walk into the exam feeling completely prepared and still fail. The feeling of knowing and the ability to produce an answer are different things, and only the second one gets marked. If your child seems to be working hard and getting nowhere, this is almost always why.
</Callout>

## The cost question: tutors and practice tools

Private tutoring in New Zealand commonly runs to tens of dollars an hour, and for a student who needs one specific thing explained by a human, a few sessions can be worth it. The limit is that a tutor is one hour a week. The other six days, the student is on their own.

What a student actually needs most of the week is the retrieval loop above: get a question, attempt it, get it marked honestly, find out what went wrong, try again. That loop is the whole engine, and it doesn't need a person in the room for every turn.

NZQA publishes past exam papers and marking schedules for free on its website. Those are the best free resource available, and every NCEA student should be using them, with the limitation that the student has to mark their own work, and self-marking is famously generous.

## What to do this week

Three things:

1. Agree a daily study slot with your child. Short, fixed, theirs to fill.
2. Ask "what did you practise today?" once, and listen to the answer.
3. Ask to see one marked practice question. If there isn't one, that's your one job for the week: help them find questions to attempt.

You don't need to know the content. You need to make the conditions right and check that the practice is real. That's the whole job, and it's a job most students would be quietly grateful for.
