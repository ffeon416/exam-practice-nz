**Most study timetables are abandoned within three days of being made.** You know the pattern. Sunday night, fresh page, colour-coded blocks from 4pm to 9pm every weeknight. Monday goes fine. Tuesday you're 40 minutes behind. Wednesday you skip a block, feel guilty, and the whole thing quietly dies.

The timetable didn't fail because you're lazy. It failed because it was built wrong. Here's how to build one that survives contact with a real week.

## Why timetables die

Three design mistakes kill almost every student timetable.

**It's too ambitious.** You planned for the best version of yourself — the one who isn't tired after school, doesn't have training on Thursday, and never gets distracted. That person doesn't exist. A timetable built for them is a timetable built to fail.

**It has no slack.** Every block is full. So when one block runs over (and one always does), everything after it collapses. There's nowhere for the overflow to go, so it goes nowhere.

**The blocks say "study X" instead of naming a task.** "Study chemistry, 5–6pm" is not a plan. It's a wish. At 5pm you'll open the folder, stare at it, spend fifteen minutes deciding what to do, and end up re-reading the first chapter. A block that says "do 5 rates-of-reaction problems, then check" gets done.

Fix those three and the timetable holds.

## The five-step build

Do this in order. It takes about 30 minutes.

### Step 1: Write down your fixed commitments first

School hours. Work. Sport. Family stuff. Travel time. Sleep — a real amount, not the amount you wish you needed.

Block these out before anything else. The study time is whatever's left, not whatever you hope for. Most students find they have 8–14 usable hours a week outside school, not the 25 they imagined. That's fine. Fourteen well-used hours beats twenty-five planned and eight delivered.

### Step 2: Put in your exam dates and count backwards

Every exam goes on the calendar. Then look at what's nearest. The subject with the exam in two weeks gets more blocks now than the subject with the exam in six weeks — even if the six-week subject is the one you're worse at.

If you're sitting a system with a fixed external exam period (NCEA in New Zealand, QCE in Queensland, GCSEs and A-levels in the UK), get the official timetable from your exam board as soon as it's published and copy every date in. Don't plan from memory.

### Step 3: Order your topics weakest-first

Within each subject, list the topics. Mark each one honestly: solid, shaky, or no idea.

The "no idea" and "shaky" topics get scheduled first and most often. The "solid" ones get a single review block near the exam. This feels backwards — everyone wants to start with what they're good at, because it's pleasant — but pleasant study is usually wasted study. If you're not sure which is which, a marked practice paper will tell you faster than guessing. [Here's how to use past papers for that](/blog/how-to-use-past-papers-mock-exam-strategy).

### Step 4: Make every block a 25–45 minute task

Not "study biology". A task with a finish line:

- "Do 4 genetics problems from the Year 12 set, mark them, write down what I got wrong"
- "Write a 10-minute plan for an essay on the causes of the Depression, no notes"
- "Recall the 12 key terms from Topic 3 onto a blank page, then check"

Keep blocks between 25 and 45 minutes. Shorter than that and you don't get into it; longer and your attention drops off without you noticing. Two 40-minute blocks with a proper break between them beat one 90-minute slog.

And make most of the tasks *retrieval*: solving, writing, recalling. Re-reading and highlighting feel productive and [barely work](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading).

### Step 5: Leave one day completely free

One full day a week with nothing scheduled. Not "light study". Nothing.

This isn't a reward. It's structural. That free day is where the overflow goes when Tuesday runs late. It's also what keeps you going in week four. A timetable with no rest is a timetable with an expiry date.

<Callout type="info">
The test of a good timetable: if you follow it at 80%, does it still work? If it only works at 100%, rebuild it with fewer blocks.
</Callout>

## Sample week

Here's what a realistic week looks like for a student with school until 3:15, sport on Tuesday and Thursday evenings, a Saturday-morning job, and three subjects with exams in the next month. Blocks are 40 minutes unless noted.

| Day | Block 1 | Block 2 | Block 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mon** | Maths: 6 algebra problems, self-mark | Chemistry: recall bonding terms from blank page | — |
| **Tue** | English: plan an unfamiliar-text response (25 min) | *Sport* | — |
| **Wed** | Maths: redo Monday's wrong answers, then 4 new | Chemistry: 5 rates problems, mark honestly | English: read one past-paper question, list what the examiner wants |
| **Thu** | Chemistry: review Monday's terms again (spaced) | *Sport* | — |
| **Fri** | Maths: timed 30-min mini-paper | English: write one full paragraph, check against the exemplar | — |
| **Sat** | *Work* | Chemistry: full past paper, timed (90 min) | Mark it, log errors by type |
| **Sun** | **Free** | **Free** | **Free** |

Notice what's in there:

- Nothing on the free day.
- Tuesday and Thursday are light because of sport, not because sport was ignored.
- Every block names a task.
- The Wednesday maths block starts by redoing Monday's mistakes. That's the whole engine of improvement.
- The Chemistry terms come back on Thursday after Monday. That's [spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide) built into the week without a separate system.

Copy the shape, not the content. Your subjects and your commitments are different. The structure is the transferable part.

## How to recover after missing days

You will miss days. Everyone does. What matters is what happens next, and the wrong response is the common one: feeling guilty, "catching up" by cramming two days into one, burning out, and quitting.

Do this instead.

**Don't try to catch up. Skip forward.** The missed blocks are gone. Rejoin the timetable at today's blocks. If a missed task was genuinely important — a weak topic that needs work — swap it into your next free-day-adjacent slot or replace the least important upcoming block with it. Everything else, let go.

**If you've missed three or more days, rebuild the week.** Not from scratch — just redo Step 2 and Step 3 with the days you have left. Exam dates haven't moved, so the priorities may have.

**Look at why you missed.** Not to feel bad about it, but because it's information. If you always miss the 8pm block, the 8pm block is in the wrong place. Move it or delete it. If you keep skipping one subject, you're probably avoiding it because it's hard, which means it needs *more* attention, not a nicer time slot.

**Keep the free day free.** The temptation after a bad week is to use Sunday to catch up. Don't. That's how you turn one bad week into a bad month.

<Callout type="warning">
A timetable you're 60% through and still following is worth more than a perfect one you restarted four times. Consistency compounds. Restarting resets it to zero.
</Callout>

## The one-line version

Fixed stuff first, exams on the calendar, weak topics early, every block a named task, one day off. Follow it at 80% and forgive the rest.
