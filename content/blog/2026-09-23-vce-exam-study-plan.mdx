**Most Year 12 students spend the last six weeks before VCE exams doing the wrong kind of work.** They make beautiful notes. They re-read the textbook. They watch a summary video, feel calmer, and walk into the exam having never once practised producing an answer under time pressure.

The end-of-year exams run from late October into November. That's your window. It is enough time to move a study score by a meaningful amount, but only if the weeks are used for the things that actually shift marks: retrieval practice, timed papers, and honest marking against what VCAA's examiners actually reward.

This is the plan. It's built around the two techniques with the strongest evidence behind them, practice testing and spaced practice, which Dunlosky and colleagues' 2013 review rated as the highest-utility study methods. Everything else is scheduling.

## How the exams feed your ATAR, in plain terms

You don't need to understand the maths behind scaling to study well, but you do need to know what you're aiming at.

- Each VCE study you complete at Units 3 and 4 gives you a **study score**, reported on a scale that tops out at 50. It comes from your graded assessments, which for most studies means your school-assessed coursework (SACs) plus the end-of-year exam or exams. The exam is usually the single biggest component.
- Study scores are calculated by VCAA. They are then **scaled** by the tertiary admissions centre, which adjusts each score up or down depending on how strong the group of students in that study was. Scaling is not a reward or punishment for the subject; it's an attempt to make scores from different studies comparable.
- Your scaled scores are combined into an aggregate. Your best few count in full and one or two more make a smaller contribution. An English study has to be in that top group.
- The aggregate is converted into the **ATAR**, which is a rank, not a score. It tells universities where you sit relative to everyone else in your age group, on a scale up to 99.95. [Wikipedia's ATAR page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Tertiary_Admission_Rank) has a clear overview if you want the detail.

What this means for planning: the exam is where the biggest movement in your study score is still possible, and every study that could end up in your top group deserves serious time. A subject you're already strong in can still move your ATAR if the exam lifts your study score.

Check your subjects' exam dates on the official timetable at [VCAA](https://www.vcaa.vic.edu.au/) and write them on the wall. The order matters for the last fortnight.

## What most people get wrong

**They treat notes as the product.** Notes are an input. The product is a marked answer. If a session doesn't end with you answering something and seeing what you got wrong, it wasn't study. We cover the evidence in [why practice beats re-reading](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading).

**They practise without marking.** A practice exam you don't mark against the examination report is a practice exam you didn't do. Marking is where you find out that "I sort of knew that" was actually zero marks.

**They never read the examination reports.** VCAA publishes a report for each exam that explains what students did well, where they lost marks, and what the assessors were looking for. It is the closest thing to sitting next to the marker. Most students never open one.

**They start timed practice too late.** Full exams under time pressure in the final week are stressful and teach you almost nothing you can still fix. Timed practice needs to start in week three so you have time to act on what it shows.

**They spread themselves evenly.** Six subjects, six equal blocks. That produces six average results. The plan below deliberately gives more time to the subjects where a jump is both valuable and realistic.

## Your best free resource: VCAA's own papers and reports

For every study, [VCAA](https://www.vcaa.vic.edu.au/) publishes past examinations and the matching examination reports. Use them like this.

**Past exams** tell you the shape of the paper: how many questions, what kinds, how the marks are spread, how much time you have per mark. Sit them under conditions. Don't skim them.

**Examination reports** tell you what the assessors thought. For each question they typically note the mark distribution and what distinguished a full-mark answer from a partial one. Read them for recurring errors. If three years of reports say students lose marks for not using data from the stimulus, that's a mark you can bank right now.

A practical way to use them:

1. Sit the exam, timed, closed book.
2. Mark it strictly against the report. Not against what you meant. Against what you wrote.
3. For every lost mark, write one line: the question, the topic, and the reason in the report's own words.
4. Read the report's general comments and add any recurring error you haven't made yet to your watch list.

That list of lost marks and recurring errors is your study plan for the next week. Our guide to [using past papers properly](/blog/how-to-use-past-papers-mock-exam-strategy) goes into the method in more depth.

## The six-week timetable

Six weeks, four phases. The mistake most students make is spending all six weeks in phase one.

| Week | Phase | What you're doing | Main tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Audit | Find out what you actually can't do | One past exam per study, timed, marked against the report |
| 2–3 | Rebuild | Retrieval practice on the weak topics from the audit | Blank-page recall, short question sets, examination reports as study texts |
| 4–5 | Simulate | Full exams under conditions, alternating with targeted fixes | Timed past exams, honest marking, corrections log |
| 6 | Taper | Light review, sleep, one final rehearsal per study | Corrections log, short retrieval sessions, rest |

### Week 1: audit

Sit one past exam for every study you're taking, timed, closed book. Mark each one against its examination report. Write down every topic where you lost marks and roughly how many.

This week feels bad. That's the point. You want the bad news now, while there's still time to act on it. By the end of the week you should have, per study, a list of three to six specific weak topics ranked by marks lost. Not "Area of Study 2". Something you can practise in one session.

If your school's last SACs are still running this week, do the audit for the studies whose SACs are finished and pick up the others next week. Don't skip it.

### Weeks 2 and 3: rebuild

Work through the weak-topic list using retrieval, not re-reading. For each topic:

1. Read your notes on it once. Slowly. Once.
2. Close them. On a blank page, write everything you can recall, including a worked example or a model paragraph.
3. Check what you missed. That gap is the learning.
4. Do three to five exam-style questions on the topic. Mark them. Write the correction next to each mistake.
5. Two days later, do two more questions on the same topic without looking at anything first.

Step five is what makes it stick. [Spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide) works because the second retrieval, after you've started to forget, is harder and more valuable. Rotate two topics per session rather than blocking a whole session on one.

Read the examination report's comments on each weak topic before you practise it. If the report says the common error was X, you now know what to avoid before you've even started.

### Weeks 4 and 5: simulate

Switch from topics to whole papers. For each priority study, sit a full past exam under conditions twice a week: timed, closed book, one sitting, phone in another room. Mark it against the report. Add every lost mark to the corrections log.

Between papers, go back to rebuild-mode on whatever the paper exposed. Don't just sit more exams and hope. The point of simulation is to find the errors that only appear under time pressure, like running out of time on the extended response or misreading a command term, and then fix them.

Save one past exam per study for week six.

### Week 6: taper

The last week is for consolidating and arriving rested, not for learning new content.

- One final timed past exam per study early in the week. Mark it. Fix the two or three things it exposes.
- Short daily retrieval sessions, 20 to 30 minutes, cycling through your corrections log. No new material.
- Sleep. Seven to nine hours, every night. Memory consolidation happens while you sleep, and an all-nighter costs more than it gains.
- The day before each exam: read your corrections log for that study once. Then stop.

If nerves are a problem, this is also the week to rehearse the routines in [how to beat test anxiety before exams](/blog/how-to-beat-test-anxiety-before-exams). Practise them during your timed papers so they're automatic on the day.

## The rules that make the plan work

**Mark everything, against the report.** Lenient self-marking is worse than not marking, because it tells you a topic is safe when it isn't.

**Track marks lost, not hours spent.** One page per study: topic, marks lost, date. When a topic's line stops growing, it's fixed.

**Retrieval first, notes second.** Open your notes to check, never to start. If you can't produce it on a blank page, you can't produce it in the exam.

**Weight the split.** Studies where a jump is valuable and realistic get three or four sessions a week. Studies already near your ceiling, or unlikely to make your top group, get one or two.

**Rest is scheduled.** Two rest sessions a week, on the timetable, not optional. Burning out in week four costs far more than it saves.

## A sample week (phase 2)

Here's a rebuild-phase week for a student taking English, Methods, Chemistry, Biology and Psychology, with Methods and Chemistry as priorities and Psychology already strong.

| Day | Session 1 (50 min) | Session 2 (50 min) |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Methods: weak topic A, recall + 4 questions | Chemistry: weak topic A, recall + 4 questions |
| Tuesday | English: one timed analytical paragraph, marked | Biology: weak topic A |
| Wednesday | Methods: weak topic B | Chemistry: weak topic B |
| Thursday | Methods: topics A + B, 2 questions each, no notes first | Psychology: 4 questions on weakest topic |
| Friday | Chemistry: topics A + B, 2 questions each, no notes first | Rest |
| Saturday | English: full timed essay, marked against the report | Biology: weak topic B, plus topic A from memory |
| Sunday | Read the week's corrections log, 30 min | Rest |

Twelve sessions, about ten hours. The point isn't the volume. It's that every session ends with something retrieved, marked, and corrected. ## Questions students ask

**How many hours a day?** In the rebuild and simulate phases, one or two 50-minute sessions on school days and two or three on weekends is plenty. If you're doing more than three hours a day and not improving, the problem is method, not time.

**What if I've still got SACs?** Finish them properly; they count. Run the audit for studies whose SACs are done, and slide the others back a week. The order of the phases stays the same.

**Should I do every past exam available?** For priority studies, most of them, spread across weeks one to six. For lower-priority studies, two or three is enough. Always keep one per study unseen for the final rehearsal.

**My exams are spread across three weeks. How do I plan the gaps?** Work backwards from each exam date. The two days before an exam belong to that study alone. Everything else slots around those blocks. If a low-priority exam falls first, give it its two days and then move on.

## Start today

The most common failure is waiting for a clean start. There isn't one. Download one past exam for the study that worries you most, sit it tonight under conditions, and mark it against the report. That's week one begun.
