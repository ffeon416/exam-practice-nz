**Chemistry is two subjects wearing one name, and most students only study one of them.** Half the external is calculation: moles, concentrations, enthalpy, pH. The other half is explanation: why this substance melts higher, why that reaction goes faster, what happens to an equilibrium when you change something. Students who are comfortable with numbers lose marks on the writing. Students who like the writing lose marks on the numbers. Almost nobody practises both the way the exam tests them.

This guide covers what the Level 2 and 3 chemistry externals ask, the two separate ways marks get lost, a 4-week routine that trains both halves, and two worked examples.

## What the chemistry externals actually test

The papers you sit depend on what your school has entered you for, so confirm the list with your teacher and check the timetable on [NZQA's NCEA site](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/). But the externally assessed content at each level clusters into a few recognisable areas.

**Level 2** typically has three external papers:

- **Structure, bonding and energy.** Ionic, metallic, covalent network and molecular substances. Shapes of molecules and whether they're polar. The forces between molecules. Using all of that to explain melting points, conductivity and solubility. Plus energy changes: exothermic and endothermic reactions, and enthalpy calculations.
- **Organic chemistry.** Naming and drawing alkanes, alkenes, haloalkanes, alcohols, amines and related compounds. Isomers. Reaction types (addition, substitution, elimination, oxidation) and the reagents and conditions for each. Reaction pathways where you get from one compound to another in several steps.
- **Chemical reactivity.** Rates of reaction and collision theory. Equilibrium and what shifts it. Acids and bases: strong versus weak, pH, and what's actually in the solution.

**Level 3** goes deeper on the same three strands:

- **Thermochemistry and particle properties.** More on bonding, shapes, polarity and intermolecular forces, with enthalpy and entropy used to explain why reactions happen.
- **Organic chemistry.** More functional groups, more reaction types, longer pathways, polymers, and stereoisomers.
- **Equilibrium in aqueous systems.** Equilibrium constants, weak acids and bases with their constants, buffers, solubility, and reading titration curves.

Across all of it there are two kinds of question. **Calculate** questions want a number with correct working and units. **Explain** questions want a chain of reasoning linking what a substance is made of to how it behaves. This guide treats them separately, because the ways students lose marks on them have nothing in common.

## Where the marks are lost

| Mistake | What it looks like | Which half |
|---|---|---|
| Volume in the wrong units | Using 25.0 (mL) in a formula that needs litres | Calculation |
| Skipping the mole ratio | Treating every reaction as 1:1 | Calculation |
| Rearranging under pressure | Dividing when you should multiply, once, on the last line | Calculation |
| Rounding too early, or wrong significant figures | Carrying 0.0011 when you should carry 0.00113, or giving 6 figures for 3 figure data | Calculation |
| Describing instead of explaining | "Ionic bonds are strong so it has a high melting point" | Explanation |
| Talking about the wrong bonds | Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular substance melts | Explanation |
| Only half the comparison | Explaining substance A fully and not mentioning substance B | Explanation |
| Vague organic pathways | "React it with something to make an alcohol" with no reagent or conditions | Explanation |

If your marks are going in the top half of the table, you need a checklist and repetition. If they're going in the bottom half, you need a different way of writing. Fixing one won't fix the other.

## Failure mode one: calculation slips

Chemistry calculations are not hard maths. They're a short chain of steps, and any link can break. The chain almost always runs: **what you're given, into moles, across the reaction ratio, out of moles into what's asked.**

This checklist stops the slips. Use it on every practice calculation until it's automatic.

1. **Write the equation first.** Balanced. The ratio you need is sitting in the coefficients and you can't use a ratio you haven't written.
2. **Convert units before you touch the formula.** Millilitres to litres (divide by 1000). Grams to moles (divide by molar mass). Kilojoules and joules match. Do this on its own line, so you can see it happened.
3. **Write the formula in symbols, then substitute.** n = cV, then n = 0.100 × 0.0250. If you go straight to numbers you'll rearrange wrongly one time in ten, and you won't be able to see where.
4. **Carry at least four significant figures through the working. Round once, at the end.** Match the final answer to the data in the question. If the question gives 0.100 and 25.0, it wants three figures.
5. **Sanity check the size.** A 0.1 mol L⁻¹ solution diluted a bit shouldn't come out at 12 mol L⁻¹. pH of a dilute acid should be between about 1 and 6. If the number looks wrong, it probably is.
6. **Units on the answer.** mol, g, mol L⁻¹, kJ mol⁻¹, whatever fits. A bare number is an incomplete answer.

For pH, one extra rule. pH = −log[H₃O⁺], and the reverse is [H₃O⁺] = 10^(−pH). A 0.0100 mol L⁻¹ solution of hydrochloric acid has [H₃O⁺] = 0.0100 mol L⁻¹, so pH = 2.00. Practise the reverse direction as often as the forward one; the minus sign goes missing under time pressure.

## Failure mode two: describing instead of explaining

Explain questions are marked on a chain. The marker is looking for specific links, and a description names the ends of the chain without the links in between. Compare:

- **Describing:** "Sodium chloride has strong ionic bonds so it has a high melting point."
- **Explaining:** "Sodium chloride is made of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions held in a lattice by strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Melting means separating the ions, which requires overcoming these strong attractions, so a large amount of energy is needed and the melting point is high."

Both are true. Only the second one shows the marker that you know *what the particles are*, *what holds them together*, *what has to happen for the substance to melt*, and *why that takes a lot of energy*. Those four links are what Merit and Excellence answers are made of.

A useful habit: after every sentence in an explain answer, ask "why?" If it doesn't already say why, that's the next sentence.

Three more things markers want in explanation answers:

- **Name the particle and the force precisely.** Not "bonds". Ions and ionic bonds. Molecules and intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonding, permanent dipole, temporary dipole). Atoms and covalent bonds. Metal ions and delocalised electrons.
- **In a comparison, do both substances**, then finish with a sentence that actually compares them.
- **In organic, give the reaction type, the reagent, and the conditions.** "Ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid" is a description. Adding the oxidising agent and conditions makes it an explanation.

For how graders separate Achieved, Merit and Excellence in written answers, see [what examiners look for at each grade](/blog/achieved-merit-excellence-what-examiners-look-for). The pattern is the same in chemistry: Achieved identifies, Merit links, Excellence links completely and compares.

## The 4-week routine

This assumes four to five sessions a week of about 45 minutes. If you're sitting several externals, [the 8-week NCEA plan](/blog/how-to-study-for-ncea-exams) shows how chemistry fits alongside the rest. The principle underneath it is retrieval: you learn chemistry by producing answers, not reading them. We cover why in [practice beats re-reading](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading).

### Week 1: sort your topics into two lists

- Sit one practice paper for each chemistry external, timed, nothing open. Mark it honestly using the marking schedule.
- For every lost mark, write it in one of two columns: **calculation** or **explanation**. Then note the topic.
- Most students find one column is much longer than the other. That column is where the next fortnight goes.

### Week 2: the longer column

- **If it's calculations:** pick the topic with the most lost marks. Do five questions on it, one at a time, using the six-step checklist above, checking after each. Every mistake gets written next to the question with one line on why.
- **If it's explanations:** pick the topic. Read your notes on it once. Close them. On a blank page, write the full explanation chain for two typical questions. Compare against the marking schedule and underline every link you missed. Rewrite. Repeat with a new question two days later, without looking first. This is [spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide), and it's what makes the chain stick.
- Two sessions on the biggest topic, two on the next, one on the shorter column so it doesn't rot.

### Week 3: the shorter column, and mixing

- Same routine on the other column.
- Start mixing topics inside a session: an enthalpy calculation, then a bonding explanation, then an organic pathway. The exam doesn't group by topic, so stop practising as if it does.
- Every session ends with one full-length explain question written under a rough time limit.

### Week 4: whole papers

- Two timed papers, one at the start of the week and one at the end. Mark both against the schedule. [How to use past papers properly](/blog/how-to-use-past-papers-mock-exam-strategy) covers the marking part, where most of the value is.
- Between them, go back to whatever the first paper exposed.
- Last two days: no new content. Redo your five worst questions from the month, from a blank page, running the checklist on every calculation.

<Callout type="warning">
Reading a worked example is not chemistry practice. Neither is watching one. If you can't reproduce it on a blank page, cold, you haven't learned it. This is true of both halves of the subject.
</Callout>

## Two worked examples

### Example A: a concentration calculation

**25.0 mL of 0.100 mol L⁻¹ sodium hydroxide solution exactly neutralises 20.0 mL of sulfuric acid solution. Calculate the concentration of the sulfuric acid.**

A common weak answer:

```
c = 0.100 × 25.0 ÷ 20.0 = 0.125 mol L⁻¹
```

The number is wrong, and there's nothing to credit: no equation, no moles, and no sign the student knows the two solutions react in anything other than a 1:1 ratio.

Full-marks working:

```
H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

n(NaOH) = cV
        = 0.100 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0250 L
        = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol

From the equation, 1 mol H₂SO₄ reacts with 2 mol NaOH,
so n(H₂SO₄) = 2.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2 = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol

c(H₂SO₄) = n ÷ V
         = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0200 L
         = 0.0625 mol L⁻¹
```

Check: sulfuric acid supplies two hydrogen ions per molecule, so fewer moles of it are needed than of sodium hydroxide, and a concentration a bit below 0.100 mol L⁻¹ is plausible. Three significant figures matches the data. Every step of the checklist is visible: equation, unit conversion, formula in symbols, ratio, answer with units. A slip on the last division would still leave the method there to credit.

### Example B: an explain question on melting points

**Sodium chloride and iodine are both solids at room temperature, but sodium chloride has a much higher melting point. Explain why, in terms of the particles and forces present in each substance.**

A weak answer:

> Sodium chloride is ionic so it has strong bonds that need a lot of energy to break. Iodine is covalent so the bonds are weaker and it melts more easily.

This gets little credit, and the second sentence is wrong. Covalent bonds are strong. The mistake is thinking the covalent bonds inside iodine molecules break when iodine melts. They don't.

What the examiner wanted:

> Sodium chloride is an ionic solid. It consists of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions arranged in a lattice, held together by strong electrostatic attractions (ionic bonds) between oppositely charged ions acting in all directions. To melt it, these strong attractions between ions must be overcome so the ions can move past each other. That requires a large amount of energy, so the melting point is high.
>
> Iodine is a molecular solid made of I₂ molecules. Within each molecule the two iodine atoms are held by a strong covalent bond, but this bond is not broken on melting. What must be overcome are the weak intermolecular forces (temporary dipole attractions) between the I₂ molecules. These are much weaker than ionic bonds, so much less energy is needed and iodine melts at a far lower temperature.
>
> The difference in melting point is therefore because melting sodium chloride requires overcoming strong ionic bonds throughout the lattice, while melting iodine only requires overcoming weak forces between molecules.

Count the links for each substance: what the particles are, what holds them together, what must be overcome to melt, and why that takes a lot or a little energy. Then a sentence that compares. The weak answer had one link per substance. The full answer has four, plus the comparison, and it gets the bonds right.

## Questions students ask

**Do I need to memorise every equation?** Know the handful of relationships cold: n = m/M, n = cV, pH = −log[H₃O⁺], and whatever your course uses for enthalpy and equilibrium constants. Knowing them isn't the hard part. Using them in the right order under time pressure is, and that comes from repetition.

**How much should I write for an explain question?** Enough to complete the chain for every substance or change in the question, and no more. Three to six sentences is typical. A full page usually means you've repeated yourself or drifted off the question.

**Is it worth learning the organic pathways off by heart?** Yes, but as a map you can draw from memory, not a list. On a blank page, draw every compound type you've covered with arrows between them, and write the reagent and conditions on each arrow. Redo it from scratch a week later. When you can draw the whole thing cold, pathway questions become a matter of tracing a route.

**I'm doing Level 3. Do the Level 2 calculation habits still matter?** More, not less. Level 3 adds places to slip (acid constants, solubility products, enthalpy cycles). If unit conversions and rearranging aren't automatic, fix that first.

## What to do today

Take one calculation and one explain question from a topic you're unsure of. Do the calculation with the six-step checklist, every line written. Write the explain answer, then read it back asking "why?" after each sentence. Mark both honestly. Thirty minutes, and you'll know which half of chemistry you need to work on.
