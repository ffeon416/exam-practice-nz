**Most students who get Merit in NCEA English are not far from Excellence. They're one sentence per paragraph away.** They know the text. They can find the quote. They can name the technique. What they can't do, yet, is say why it matters beyond the page.

That gap has a name in the standards. Achieved is *describe*. Merit is *convincing*. Excellence is *perceptive*. Once you understand what those three words are actually asking you to do, the path from Merit to Excellence stops being a mystery and becomes a checklist.

## The verb ladder: Achieved, Merit, Excellence

NCEA English standards (both the essay-style responses and the unfamiliar-text papers) grade on a ladder of thinking, and the ladder is written in the verbs.

| Grade | What the standard asks for | What it looks like on the page |
|---|---|---|
| Achieved | Identify, describe | "The author uses a metaphor here. It compares the city to a machine." |
| Merit | Explain convincingly | "The machine metaphor shows the city as cold and repetitive, which reflects how the narrator feels trapped by routine." |
| Excellence | Perceptively, with insight | "The machine metaphor does more than show the narrator's boredom. It suggests the author sees modern city life as something that consumes people as parts. That's why the ending, where he walks out, reads as an escape rather than a breakdown, and it's why the story still lands for anyone who has felt like a cog." |

Read the three examples again. Each one contains the one before it. Excellence isn't a different kind of answer. It's the Merit answer plus two more moves:

1. **A link to the author's purpose.** Not just what the technique does to the reader, but why the writer chose it. What are they trying to say about people, or the world?
2. **A step beyond the text.** A connection to the wider world, to the reader's own experience, to how the idea plays out in real life, or to a bigger truth the text is pointing at.

"Perceptive" means you noticed something that wasn't on the surface, and "insight" means you can explain it in a way that makes the reader of your essay see the text differently. That's it. It is not about using bigger words.

## A paragraph structure built for Excellence

Most students learn a version of PEE (point, evidence, explanation) in Year 9 and never upgrade it. PEE tops out at Merit. Here's the version that reaches Excellence.

**Point.** One clear claim that answers the question. Not a description of what happens, but an argument about it.

**Evidence.** A short, precise quote. Two lines at most. Long quotes waste words and hide whether you understood them.

**Technique.** Name the technique in the quote. Metaphor, juxtaposition, short sentence, second-person address, whatever it is.

**Effect.** What the technique does. What it makes the reader see, feel or understand.

**Purpose.** Why the author did that. What idea about people or society are they building?

**Beyond.** One or two sentences that step outside the text. Why does this matter to a reader in Aotearoa now? What does it reveal about how people actually behave? Where else have you seen this idea play out?

**Link.** A closing sentence that ties the paragraph back to the question.

Seven moves sounds like a lot. In practice, purpose and beyond are often a single sentence each, and the link is half a line. The paragraph gets longer by maybe three sentences. Those three sentences are the difference between M and E.

<Callout type="info">
Test your paragraph with one question: "Could this paragraph have been written by someone who only read the SparkNotes summary?" If yes, you're describing. Add the purpose and the beyond sentences until the answer is no.
</Callout>

## How to plan an essay in five minutes

The students who run out of time in English are almost never slow writers. They're students who started writing before they knew what they were arguing. Five minutes of planning saves fifteen minutes of wandering.

1. **Minute one: pick the question and underline the verbs.** "Analyse how the writer uses setting to reveal character." The verbs and nouns tell you exactly what every paragraph has to do.
2. **Minute two: write your thesis in one sentence.** This is your answer to the question. If you can't write it, you're not ready to start. "In *[text]*, the writer uses the isolated farmhouse to show that Ana's independence is really a fear of being known."
3. **Minute three: choose three moments.** Three quotes or scenes that prove the thesis. Write them as three words each. You need them to be different from each other, ideally showing a change or development across the text.
4. **Minute four: for each moment, write the technique and the purpose.** Two words each. This is where you check you actually have an Excellence-level idea for each paragraph, not just a quote.
5. **Minute five: write the "beyond" idea.** One line about why this matters outside the text. You'll use this in your conclusion and echo it in at least one body paragraph.

Now write. Because the argument is already decided, the writing is just filling in the structure, and you'll finish with time to reread.

## The unfamiliar-text paper

The unfamiliar-text exam gives you passages you've never seen and asks how the writer creates effect. Students often treat it as easier than the essay because there's nothing to memorise. It isn't easier. It's the same ladder, under time pressure, with no chance to prepare specific quotes.

The approach:

- **Read the question before the text.** You're reading for a purpose, so know what it is.
- **Read the text twice.** First for meaning. Second with a pen, marking every technique you notice and one word about its effect in the margin.
- **Answer the actual question.** If it asks how the writer creates a sense of unease, every technique you discuss has to connect to unease. Naming three techniques that have nothing to do with the question is an Achieved answer at best.
- **Use the same paragraph structure.** Point, evidence, technique, effect, purpose, beyond. Yes, even here. "The writer is likely doing this to make readers question how much they trust their own memory" is a purpose sentence, and it lifts you from Merit.
- **Quote precisely.** Two or three words in quotation marks, with the technique named, beats a whole sentence copied out.

Unfamiliar-text practice is where honest marking matters most, because it's easy to feel like you did well. ## Five reasons students stall at Merit

Having seen a lot of Merit essays, these are the patterns. If you recognise yourself in one, that's your fix.

### 1. The paragraphs describe the text instead of arguing about it

"In this scene, Kahu swims out to the whale." That's a plot summary. The marker has read the book. Every sentence in your essay should be something the marker couldn't get from reading the text themselves.

### 2. Techniques are named but not explained

"The author uses a simile." Fine. So what? What does the comparison make you see, and why did the author want you to see that?

### 3. No author's purpose, anywhere

Merit essays talk about what the text does. Excellence essays talk about what the *writer* is doing and why. Get in the habit of writing "the writer wants us to..." at least once per paragraph.

### 4. The "beyond" is generic

"This is relevant today because people still experience loss." True and empty. A real "beyond" is specific: "This is why the ending works on readers who grew up between two cultures, because the text refuses to let Kahu choose one."

### 5. The conclusion just repeats the introduction

The conclusion is your last chance to be perceptive. Don't summarise. Say the thing the whole essay has been building towards, in one or two sentences that go slightly further than any body paragraph did.

## What to practise this week

Write one paragraph a day. Not an essay. One paragraph, using the seven-move structure, on a different question each time. Time it: ten minutes. Then reread it and ask the SparkNotes question.

By Friday you'll have five paragraphs and you'll know exactly where your writing sits on the ladder. A week later, do a full timed essay and see how much of it comes automatically.
