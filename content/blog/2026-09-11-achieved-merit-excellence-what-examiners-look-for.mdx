**Most students who get Merit knew enough for Excellence. They just didn't write it in the form the marker was told to look for.** The knowledge was there. The answer wasn't.

NCEA marking isn't a mystery. Every achievement standard publishes exactly what Achieved, Merit and Excellence require, and the difference between the bands is almost always the same pattern, whatever the subject. Once you can see the pattern, you can aim your answers at a band on purpose instead of hoping.

## The verb ladder

Open any achievement standard and look at the three columns. The content is the same across all three. What changes is the verb.

| Band | Typical verbs | What the marker is checking |
|---|---|---|
| Achieved | describe, identify, state, apply, show | Do you know what it is and can you do the basic thing? |
| Merit | explain, relate, apply in depth, compare, discuss | Do you understand *why*, and can you connect two things? |
| Excellence | justify, evaluate, analyse, discuss comprehensively, show insight, extended abstract thinking | Can you make a judgement, defend it, and link it to the bigger picture? |

The wording varies by subject. Maths standards talk about "apply", "relational thinking" and "extended abstract thinking". Sciences and humanities use "describe", "explain" and "discuss" or "evaluate". English uses "show understanding", "convincing" and "perceptive". But the ladder underneath is always the same three rungs:

1. **Achieved: what.** Correct facts, correct method, correct identification.
2. **Merit: why and how.** Reasons, links, mechanisms, working shown and explained.
3. **Excellence: so what.** Judgement, justification, weighing alternatives, connecting to the wider question.

The examiner is reading your answer with a checklist of these verbs in mind. If the question says "discuss" and your answer only describes, you have capped yourself at Achieved before the marker has read a word of the content.

## A maths-style example

Take a Level 1 question that gives you a rectangle with a perimeter of 30 cm, where the length is 3 cm more than the width, and asks you to find the dimensions and then decide whether a design constraint (say, the area must be at least 50 cm²) is met.

**Achieved-level answer:**

> Width = 6, length = 9. Area = 54.

Correct numbers. No working, no reasoning, no engagement with the "decide" part beyond the number. That's applying a method. It's Achieved, and depending on the standard it might not even hold Achieved without the working.

**Merit-level answer:**

> Let width = w. Then length = w + 3. Perimeter: 2w + 2(w + 3) = 30, so 4w + 6 = 30, 4w = 24, w = 6. Length = 9. Area = 6 × 9 = 54 cm². 54 is greater than 50, so the constraint is met.

Now the marker can see the relationship being set up, the equation being solved, and the result being checked against the condition. That's relational thinking: the parts are connected. Merit.

**Excellence-level answer:**

> Let width = w, length = w + 3. From the perimeter, 4w + 6 = 30, so w = 6 and length = 9. Area = 54 cm², which meets the 50 cm² minimum with 4 cm² to spare. The margin is small: if the perimeter were fixed at 30 but the length were only 1 cm more than the width, the dimensions would be 7 × 8 and the area 56, so a squarer shape gives more area for the same perimeter. The design meets the constraint, but any increase in the length-width gap beyond about 5 cm would fail it, because area falls as the rectangle gets longer and thinner at fixed perimeter.

Same maths. What's been added is a generalisation (squarer means more area at fixed perimeter), a sensitivity check (how much can the gap grow before it fails), and a justified conclusion. That's extended abstract thinking: the student stepped outside the specific numbers and said something true about the whole situation. Excellence.

Notice the Excellence answer isn't longer because it rambles. Every extra sentence does a job the standard names.

## An English-style example

A Level 2 unfamiliar-text question quotes a short passage where the writer describes a childhood kitchen and asks you to analyse how the writer uses language to convey a sense of loss.

**Achieved-level answer:**

> The writer uses imagery. "The kettle still sat where she'd left it" is an image of the kitchen. This shows the person is gone and the writer misses them.

Technique identified. Example quoted. Effect stated. It's correct and it's Achieved. The marker can tick "identifies language feature" and "describes effect", and nothing more.

**Merit-level answer:**

> The writer uses the image "the kettle still sat where she'd left it" to convey loss. The word "still" suggests time has stopped in the kitchen, as if nothing has moved since the person died, which makes the reader feel the writer can't move on either. Because the kettle is such an ordinary object, the loss feels everyday and constant rather than dramatic, which is more painful.

Now there's an explanation of *how* the language works. A specific word is unpacked. The effect is linked to the reader's experience. That's the "convincing" understanding Merit asks for.

**Excellence-level answer:**

> The writer conveys loss by refusing to name it. "The kettle still sat where she'd left it" places the emphasis on an ordinary object rather than on the person, and the word "still" does the emotional work: it freezes the kitchen at the moment of her leaving, so that time has passed for the writer but not for the room. That gap is the loss. By keeping the language plain and domestic, the writer suggests grief isn't a single dramatic event but a series of small collisions with things that haven't changed, which is why the passage is more affecting than an outright statement of sadness would be. The restraint is the technique: the writer trusts the reader to feel what isn't said.

The Excellence answer makes an argument about the writer's choices, evaluates why this approach works better than the alternative, and connects the detail to the passage as a whole. That's perceptive. The student isn't just reporting what the language does; they're judging why the writer chose it.

## The five ways students leave a band on the table

Once you know the ladder, the mistakes are easy to name. These are the ones markers see constantly.

### 1. Answering the question they wish was asked

The question says "evaluate". The student explains. The explanation is excellent. It's Merit, because "evaluate" needs a judgement and none was made. Before you write, underline the verb. Then make sure your answer does that verb, explicitly, at least once.

### 2. Stopping at the answer

In maths and science especially: the number is right, the working is missing, and the mark scheme wanted to see the reasoning. Achieved needs the correct method visible. Merit needs the steps connected. Write the working even when you can do it in your head. A right answer with no working can score zero on a standard that assesses the method.

### 3. No "because"

Merit is the band of reasons. If your sentences don't contain "because", "which means", "so that", or "as a result", you're describing, not explaining. Go back through a practice answer and count them. Fewer than one per paragraph is an Achieved answer.

### 4. No judgement

Excellence needs you to take a position and defend it. "There are advantages and disadvantages to both" is not a judgement. "Option B is better here because the cost of A's main disadvantage outweighs its benefit in this context" is. Say which. Say why. Say what would change your mind.

### 5. No link back

The last thing an Excellence answer does is connect the specific point to the whole question, the whole text, or the whole situation. Students who've done the analysis often finish without saying what it adds up to. One closing sentence that answers "so what does this tell us overall?" is often the difference between Merit and Excellence.

<Callout type="warning">
Writing more is not the fix. Markers are not counting words. A long answer that describes six things is still Achieved. A short answer that explains one thing and justifies a conclusion about it can be Excellence. Aim at the verb, not the word count.
</Callout>

## How to practise for a band

You can't practise for Excellence by reading about it. You practise by writing answers and having them marked against the standard, band by band, until the higher band is what you produce automatically.

1. **Read the standard's Achieved, Merit and Excellence descriptors** for each external you're sitting. They're published on [NZQA's NCEA site](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/) with the exam papers and assessment schedules. Copy the key verbs onto one page per subject.
2. **Write an answer to a practice question at the band you're currently getting.** Then rewrite it one band up, using the ladder: add the "because", add the judgement, add the link back.
3. **Get it marked honestly.** Not "that's pretty good." Which band, and what specifically was missing for the next one. If you're marking yourself, use the assessment schedule and be harsher than feels fair.
4. **Repeat on your weak topics, spaced out over the weeks.** The [8-week NCEA study plan](/blog/how-to-study-for-ncea-exams) sets out how to cycle this so the improvement sticks by November.

If you're deciding which subjects are worth this effort, the [endorsement guide](/blog/ncea-endorsement-merit-excellence) covers how to pick the externals where one band up matters most.

## The short version

Achieved is what. Merit is why. Excellence is so what. Underline the verb, do the verb, show the working, give the reason, make the call, link it back. Then practise until it's how you write by default.
