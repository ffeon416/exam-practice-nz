**Most students use past papers the way they'd use a textbook: read the question, glance at the answer, nod, move on.** Then they walk into the exam having "done" six past papers and get a grade that doesn't reflect any of that work.

Past papers are the single best free resource in exam preparation. They're written by the people who write your exam, in the format your exam uses, at the level your exam is set. Every exam board publishes them: NZQA for [NCEA](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/), [QCAA](https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/) for Queensland, and the equivalent bodies for the HSC, GCSEs and A-levels.

The problem isn't access. It's method. This guide gives you the method.

## The three ways students waste past papers

### Reading the answers first

You open the paper, read a question, aren't sure, flick to the marking schedule, read the answer, think "yeah, that makes sense", and move on. You've now taught yourself to *recognise* the answer. You have not taught yourself to *produce* it, and producing it is the only thing the exam tests.

This feels like study. It's closer to watching someone else study.

### Doing them untimed

You work through the paper over two evenings, with your notes open, taking breaks when it gets hard. You get most of it right. In the exam, you have 3 hours, no notes, and a question that looks slightly different from the one you practised. The untimed version measured your knowledge under ideal conditions. The exam doesn't run under ideal conditions.

### Never marking honestly

You check your answers, see that you got "basically" the right idea, and give yourself the mark. The examiner won't. "Basically right" in an exam hall is worth exactly what the schedule says it's worth, which is often nothing. Lenient self-marking is the most common reason a student's practice scores don't predict their real score.

<Callout type="warning">
If your past-paper scores are consistently higher than your test scores, one of these three things is happening. Usually all three.
</Callout>

## The four-phase method

Every past paper you do should go through these four phases, in order. The first phase is optional once you're confident. The other three never are.

### Phase 1: Open-book learning pass

*Use this when you're still learning the topic, not when you're testing it.*

Work through the paper with your notes open and no timer. The goal isn't a score. It's to see how the exam asks about the topic — what command words it uses, how much working it expects, what a "discuss" question actually wants versus an "explain" question.

Write down anything you had to look up. That's a gap. It goes on your review list.

Do this once or twice per subject early in your preparation. After that, stop. Open-book papers stop teaching you anything new fairly quickly, and they're actively harmful if they become your default, because they inflate your confidence.

### Phase 2: Timed, closed-book, exam conditions

This is the one that matters.

Real time limit. No notes. No phone. Sit at a desk, not on your bed. If the exam is three hours, this is three hours. If you'd normally leave early, don't — use the spare time to check, like you would in the real thing.

Do the whole paper, including the questions you can't do. Write *something* for those: the formula you think applies, the first step, a diagram. Partial working earns partial marks in most schemes, and the habit of attempting everything is worth building now.

This will feel worse than Phase 1. That's the point. You're finding out what you can actually do under exam pressure, which is the only thing worth finding out.

### Phase 3: Self-mark with zero leniency

Get the official marking schedule. Mark your paper the way the examiner would.

The rule: **if it isn't on the page, you didn't get the mark.** Not "I knew that". Not "I would have written it if I'd had more time". What's written, at the standard the schedule demands, or nothing.

Some specific traps:

- **Method marks need the method shown.** A correct final answer with no working often gets only the answer mark. Check what your scheme awards for working and mark yourself accordingly.
- **"Explain" means a reason, not a restatement.** If the schedule wants a cause and you gave a description, it's wrong.
- **Hedging is zero.** "It could be A or B" is not an answer. If you wrote two answers hoping one lands, mark it as if you wrote neither, because that's what the examiner will do.
- **Read the exemplars.** Many boards publish annotated student responses at each grade. They show you exactly what the boundary between grades looks like. Use them.

Write your score at the top. Then write what grade that score would be. This is your real position right now.

### Phase 4: Error log by type

This is the phase that separates students who improve from students who just do a lot of papers.

For every mark you lost, write one line in a running log with the topic and *why* you lost it. Not the question number — the reason. Nearly every lost mark falls into one of five types:

| Error type | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge gap** | Didn't know the content | Go back and learn it, then [space the review](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide) |
| **Method error** | Knew the content, applied it wrong | Do 5 more of that question type this week |
| **Misread question** | Answered something the question didn't ask | Underline command words before starting every question |
| **Incomplete answer** | Right idea, not enough of it for the marks | Check the mark allocation; 4 marks needs 4 things |
| **Time** | Ran out before finishing | Practise the paper in sections with a per-question time budget |

After three or four papers, look at the log. One or two error types will dominate. That's your priority list. A student losing most marks to "incomplete answer" doesn't need to learn more content — they need to write more per question. A student losing them to "knowledge gap" needs the opposite.

Fix the biggest type first. Then do another paper and see if it moved.

## How many papers is enough

More than one, fewer than you think.

For most subjects, four to six full papers run through all four phases will teach you more than fifteen papers skimmed. The value is in Phases 3 and 4, and those take time. If you're doing papers faster than you're logging and fixing the errors from them, slow down.

A reasonable target for a subject with an external exam: one full timed paper a week for the last six weeks, with the error log driving what you study between them. Closer to the exam, you might do two in the final week, one of them a few days out so you have time to patch the last gaps.

## When you run out of papers

You will. Most boards publish a few years of papers, and if you're on a new or recently changed syllabus there might be only one or two that match what you'll sit. You'll also find that after the third time through the same paper, you're remembering the answers rather than solving the questions.

Options, roughly in order of usefulness:

1. **Papers from adjacent years or the old syllabus**, if the content mostly overlaps. Skip the questions on removed topics.
2. **Questions from the same board's other levels**, where the topic is shared. A Level 2 question on a topic that's also in Level 3 is still useful practice.
3. **Textbook end-of-chapter questions**, done timed and marked strictly. Less exam-like, but fine for method errors.
4. **AI-generated exam-style practice.** This is what StudyAce does: it generates unlimited new exam-style papers for your subject and level, marked honestly — one mark for working, one for the answer, and "not sure" scores zero. It won't replace your board's official papers, and it isn't meant to. It gives you fresh questions in the right format when the official supply runs out, so you can keep doing Phases 2 through 4 instead of memorising the same six papers.

Whatever you use, keep the method the same. Timed, closed-book, marked without mercy, errors logged by type.

## The mock exam week plan

If you've got a school mock exam or a real exam inside the next fortnight, here's the compressed version:

- **7 days out:** one full timed paper. Mark it. Log it. Identify the top two error types.
- **6–4 days out:** targeted work on those two types only. Short, focused blocks. No new papers.
- **3 days out:** second full timed paper. Mark it. Did the top two errors shrink? If yes, move to error type three. If no, keep going on the same two.
- **2 days out:** review the error log, redo the specific questions you lost most marks on, and [sort your sleep out](/blog/how-to-beat-test-anxiety-before-exams).
- **1 day out:** light retrieval only. No full papers. Nothing new.

That's two papers, properly used, in a week. It'll do more than six papers skimmed.
