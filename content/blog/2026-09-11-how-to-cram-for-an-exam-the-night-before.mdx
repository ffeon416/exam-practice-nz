**Most last-minute cramming fails because it's spent on the wrong things.** Not because cramming can't work — it can, a bit — but because the student reads the whole textbook from page one, panics at 1am, and turns up to the exam exhausted with nothing extra in their head.

Let's be honest about what you're doing. You should have started weeks ago with a [spaced schedule](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide). You didn't. That's done. What you have is roughly twelve hours, six of which need to be sleep, and the question is how to spend the other six so they count.

Here's the plan.

## First: what cramming can and can't do

Cramming loads information into short-term memory. Much of it will be gone in a few days. That doesn't matter tonight, because the exam is tomorrow — you only need it to last until about midday.

What cramming *can* do in an evening:

- Refresh topics you've already learned once but let slide
- Nail down formulas, definitions, key dates and structures
- Fix a specific method you keep getting wrong
- Get you familiar with the exam format so nothing surprises you

What it can't do:

- Teach you a topic from nothing
- Replace practice on questions you've never attempted
- Work if you don't sleep

If you go in expecting the first list, tonight is useful. If you're expecting the second, you'll waste it.

## Triage: pick the topics that pay

You can't cover everything. Don't try. Sort the topics like this.

**Half-known topics with lots of marks.** These are the jackpot. You've seen them before, you half remember them, and the exam weights them heavily. An hour here can turn a shaky Achieved into a Merit, or a C into a B. Do these first.

**Well-known topics with lots of marks.** A quick review only — ten or fifteen minutes to confirm they're still there. Don't spend an hour polishing what's already fine.

**Half-known topics with few marks.** If there's time. Usually there isn't.

**Unknown topics.** Skip them. Seriously. A topic you've never learned is not going to be learned tonight to a standard that scores, and every minute spent on it is a minute stolen from a topic that would have. If it comes up in the exam, write what you can and move on.

Look at the exam structure if you know it — how many marks per section, which topics are compulsory. That tells you where the marks are, and where the marks are is where tonight goes.

<Callout type="info">
The rule: highest marks × most half-known. If you only remember one thing from this post, make it that.
</Callout>

## The six-hour plan

Say you get home at 4pm and the exam is at 9am tomorrow. Here's the evening.

**4:00–4:30 — Set up.** Get the exam format in front of you. List every topic with a marks estimate and a "know it / half know it / don't know it" rating. Pick your top three or four half-known, high-mark topics. Write them down in order. Put your phone in another room.

**4:30–5:30 — Topic 1.** Don't re-read the chapter. Get a summary sheet or your notes, cover it, and try to write out the key points from memory. Check. Fill the gaps. Then do two or three questions on it — actual questions, from a past paper or a textbook, with a strict self-mark. Wrong answers get fixed now, not noted for later.

**5:30–5:45 — Break.** Eat something. Move.

**5:45–6:45 — Topic 2.** Same method. Recall first, check second, questions third.

**6:45–7:30 — Dinner.** A proper one. You'll study better fed.

**7:30–8:30 — Topic 3.** Same again. If you're flagging, cut this to 40 minutes and don't feel bad.

**8:30–9:15 — The list.** Formulas, definitions, dates, diagrams, structures — everything that's pure recall. Write them all out on one page from memory. Check. Rewrite the ones you got wrong. Then rewrite those again. This is the one place where rote repetition tonight is worth it.

**9:15–9:45 — Format run-through.** Look at the front of a past paper. Read the instructions. Know how many questions, how long each one gets, whether you get choice. Decide your time budget per section now, so you're not working it out in the hall.

**9:45 — Stop.** Put everything away. You're done.

That's four topics plus the recall list in under six hours, with breaks. It's not comprehensive. It's targeted, which is better.

## Sleep is not optional

This is the part people skip, and it's the part that decides whether the evening worked.

Memory consolidation — the process where what you studied gets stabilised into something you can recall tomorrow — happens during sleep. This is well-established, not a study tip. If you cram until 3am and sleep four hours, you're cutting the process that turns tonight's work into tomorrow's marks. You'll also read slower, make more careless errors, and misjudge time in the exam.

Seven hours minimum. Eight if you can. That means lights out by 10:30 for a 9am exam with a 7am alarm. Set the alarm before you sit down at 4pm so it's decided and you don't negotiate with yourself at 10:15.

An all-nighter almost always scores worse than the same student would have with three fewer hours of study and seven more of sleep. That's the trade you're making if you push through.

<Callout type="warning">
If you're choosing between one more topic and one more hour of sleep, take the sleep. Every time.
</Callout>

## The morning

**Wake up with enough time to not rush.** Rushing spikes stress, and stress wrecks recall. Aim to be at the exam venue 20 minutes early.

**Eat breakfast.** Something with actual substance. Skip the energy drink; the crash lands mid-exam.

**Do ten minutes of the recall list.** Not the topics — just the one-page list of formulas and definitions from 8:30 last night. Cover, recall, check. This primes what's freshest.

**Then stop.** No new material. No frantic re-reading in the corridor. Whatever's in your head is what's going in. Reading new things in the last twenty minutes mostly adds noise and anxiety — [here's how to handle the nerves](/blog/how-to-beat-test-anxiety-before-exams) if they're bad.

**In the exam, use the time budget you set last night.** Attempt everything. If a question is on a topic you skipped, write whatever partial working or reasoning you can and move on. Blank pages score zero; a half-attempt often doesn't.

## What to never do the night before

**The all-nighter.** Covered. It doesn't work.

**Reading the whole textbook.** You cannot read 200 pages into memory in an evening. You can barely read 200 pages in an evening. Start with summaries and questions, not chapters.

**Starting a new topic at midnight.** If you haven't learned it by 10pm, it's not getting learned tonight. Sleep.

**Passive review.** Watching videos, re-reading notes, highlighting. It feels like study and produces [almost nothing you can retrieve tomorrow](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading). Every block tonight should involve you producing answers — writing from memory, solving problems, self-marking.

**Group chat "study".** Someone will send a question at 11pm that you can't answer, and you'll spend 40 minutes on it in a panic. Phone in the other room. You can compare notes after the exam.

**Studying in bed.** You'll fall asleep on the notes, or you'll train your brain that bed is for cramming and then not sleep. Desk until 9:45, bed after.

## Next time

Tonight's plan is damage control. It's worth doing well, and if you follow it you'll likely score better than you would have. But the students getting the top grades aren't cramming — they've been doing short, spaced, marked practice for weeks and tonight is a light review.

After the exam, when the relief wears off, [build the timetable](/blog/how-to-make-a-study-timetable-you-will-follow). Start it the week after. It's a lot less stressful than this.
