**Most Year 11 students revise for their mocks by reading their notes from the start of the course and hoping they reach the end before the first paper.** They don't. They arrive at the exam hall having "revised" three topics thoroughly, six not at all, and none of them under timed conditions.

Mocks usually land somewhere between November and January, depending on your school. That gives you a proper run-up if you start now, and a scramble if you start in half term. This is the four-week version: what mocks are actually for, how the 9 to 1 grades work, why your exam board's own past papers are the best free resource you have, and a week-by-week timetable that puts retrieval first and re-reading last.

## Why mocks matter more than most students think

Mocks are not the real thing, and everyone knows it. But three things ride on them.

**Predicted grades.** Many schools use mock results as a major input into the predicted grades that go on your sixth form or college application. A poor mock can follow you into an interview even if your summer grade turns out fine.

**Finding gaps while there's time to fix them.** A mock in December tells you what you can't do with months left to learn it. The same discovery in the real exam is just a lower grade. This is the single most useful thing a mock does, and only if you treat the marked paper as information rather than a verdict.

**Rehearsing the format.** Sitting a full paper, in silence, against the clock, with a real mark scheme applied afterwards, is a skill. Mocks are the only full rehearsal most students get before summer.

The mistake is treating a mock as a test of whether you're clever. It's a test of what you've practised. Which means it's fixable.

## The 9 to 1 grades, in one paragraph

GCSEs in England are graded from 9 (highest) down to 1, with U for ungraded. Grade 4 is described as a standard pass and grade 5 as a strong pass, and most sixth forms and colleges set entry requirements in terms of these grades (a common pattern is a minimum grade in English and maths plus higher grades in the subjects you want to study at A-level). The number of marks needed for each grade is set by the exam board after each exam series, so it moves from year to year and paper to paper. Don't plan around a fixed number of marks you heard from an older sibling. Plan around getting more questions right than last time. The [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/) site has the official description of the grading scale if you want it from the source.

## Why your exam board's past papers are the best free resource you have

Every exam board publishes past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports for free. [AQA's site](https://www.aqa.org.uk/) is one; Edexcel (Pearson) and OCR have the same. Find out which board sets each of your subjects (it's on your school's course information, or ask your teacher) and use that board's papers, because the question styles differ.

Three things make them better than any revision guide:

- **The questions are the real format.** Command words, mark allocations, the way a six-mark question is structured. You can't get that from a textbook summary.
- **The mark scheme tells you what earns the marks.** Not what a good answer sounds like, but the specific points, working or techniques the examiner ticks. Reading a mark scheme against your own answer is the fastest way to learn how the subject is actually assessed.
- **The examiner report tells you where everyone else lost marks.** It's a list of common mistakes, written by the people who marked them. Most students never open it.

If you only use past papers for the last week, you'll waste them. The method in [how to use past papers properly](/blog/how-to-use-past-papers-mock-exam-strategy) spreads them across the whole run-up: one early to find the gaps, several in the middle for targeted practice, one late as a rehearsal.

<Callout type="info">
Mock papers are often built from past papers or the board's specimen papers. That's a good reason to have done some yourself, and a bad reason to memorise one. Learn the topic, not the paper.
</Callout>

## The 4-week revision timetable

The order matters more than the hours. You can't fix what you haven't found, and you can't perform what you haven't rehearsed under time. The whole approach is built on the finding, replicated many times, that testing yourself beats re-reading: see [why practice beats re-reading](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading) for the evidence.

| Week | Job | What you actually do | Papers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Audit | Find out what you can't do | One past paper per subject, timed, closed book. Mark with the mark scheme. List every topic that lost marks, ranked by marks lost. | 1 per subject |
| 2. Rebuild | Retrieval on the weak topics | For each weak topic: read the notes once, close them, write what you can recall on a blank page, check, then do 3 to 5 exam questions on it. Return to each topic two days later. | Topic questions only |
| 3. Practise | Mixed questions and timed sections | Mix topics inside each session. Do timed sections of papers (one long question, one full section). Mark everything against the scheme. Read the examiner report for each subject. | Half papers |
| 4. Rehearse | Full papers, then taper | One full timed paper per priority subject early in the week. Corrections. Last three days: short retrieval sessions only, no new content, proper sleep. | 1 per priority subject |

### Week 1: audit

Do not open a revision guide. Sit a full past paper for each subject you're being mocked in, under exam conditions, and mark it with the real mark scheme. Be harsh. If the scheme wants two points and you gave one, that's half the marks, not "basically right."

Then, for each subject, write the weak-topic list. Specific, not general. "Quadratic simultaneous equations" and "the effect of temperature on rate of reaction", not "algebra" and "chemistry." By Sunday you have a ranked list per subject. That list is your whole plan for the next fortnight.

### Week 2: rebuild with retrieval

Work down the weak-topic list, top first. For each topic: notes once, then blank-page recall, then exam questions from the board's papers, marked. Two topics per session, alternated.

The step people skip is the return visit. Two days after you first work a topic, do two more questions on it without looking at anything first. That second retrieval, once you've started to forget, is what makes it stay. [Spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide) explains why, and how to schedule it without a colour-coded wall chart.

### Week 3: practise under time

Now start working the way the exam works: mixed topics, against the clock. Take one section of a past paper, set a timer for the time the paper allows for it, and do it in one sitting. Mark it. Note anything that only went wrong because of the clock (rushed working, a question skipped, a misread command word). Those are different problems from not knowing the content, and they need different fixes.

This is also the week to read each subject's examiner report. It takes fifteen minutes and tells you what to avoid.

### Week 4: rehearse and taper

One full timed paper per priority subject in the first half of the week. Mark it, fix the two or three things it exposes. Then stop learning. The last three days are short retrieval sessions on your weak-topic lists, a read-through of your corrections, and early nights. If you find yourself anxious at this point, that's normal for someone who's done the work; the routines in [how to beat test anxiety](/blog/how-to-beat-test-anxiety-before-exams) are worth a read.

<Callout type="warning">
If you catch yourself highlighting, rewriting notes, or watching a revision video "to refresh" in weeks 3 or 4, stop. Those feel like revision and produce almost nothing you can retrieve in a silent hall. The only test is whether you can produce it on a blank page.
</Callout>

## The rules that make the timetable work

**Prioritise, don't spread.** Rank your subjects by how much a grade change matters (sixth form entry, A-level choices) and how close you are to the next grade. Top subjects get three or four sessions a week. Strong subjects get one. Even coverage is how you end up one grade short in everything.

**Mark with the scheme, every time.** A question you didn't mark is a question you didn't revise. Marking yourself leniently is worse, because it tells you a topic is safe when it isn't.

**Track marks lost, not hours done.** One page per subject: topic, marks lost, date. When a topic stops appearing, it's fixed.

**Sessions have names.** "Maths, 4pm" gets postponed. "Maths: 5 questions on circle theorems, marked" gets done. [How to make a study timetable you'll actually follow](/blog/how-to-make-a-study-timetable-you-will-follow) covers building one around school, clubs and a job.

**Rest is on the timetable.** Two rest sessions a week minimum. Burning out in week 3 costs more than it saves.

## A sample week (week 2)

For a student mocked in six subjects, with maths and biology as priorities, English mid-priority, and history, French and geography already comfortable.

| Day | Session 1 (45 min) | Session 2 (45 min) |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Maths: weak topic A, recall + 4 questions | Biology: weak topic A, recall + 4 questions |
| Tuesday | English: one timed essay paragraph, marked against the scheme | Maths: weak topic B |
| Wednesday | Biology: weak topic B | History: 3 questions on weakest topic |
| Thursday | Maths: topics A + B, 2 questions each, no notes first | French: vocab recall + one reading question |
| Friday | Biology: topics A + B, 2 questions each, no notes first | Rest |
| Saturday | Maths: weak topic C | Geography: 3 questions on weakest topic |
| Sunday | Review corrections from the week, 30 min | Rest |

Eleven sessions, most under an hour. It's not the volume. It's that every session ends with you retrieving something, marking it, and seeing what went wrong.

## Questions students ask

**How long should I revise each day?** In weeks 2 and 3, one or two 45-minute sessions on school days and two or three at the weekend covers most students. If you're doing more than three hours a day and the marks aren't moving, the method is wrong, not the time.

**What if I've left it until two weeks before?** Compress, don't skip. Audit in two days, rebuild for a week on your top two or three subjects, one timed paper each, then a three-day taper. The order stays the same. With only a few days left, the honest version is in [how to cram the night before](/blog/how-to-cram-for-an-exam-the-night-before).

**Do mocks count towards my actual GCSE?** For most subjects, no. The grade comes from the summer exams (and any coursework or non-exam assessment). But the predicted grades built from mocks can shape which sixth form or college you get into, so they're not nothing.

**Should I revise subjects I'm already good at?** One session a week, mostly timed questions, to stay sharp. Any more is time stolen from a subject where the grade would actually move.
