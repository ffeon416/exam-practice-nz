**Most students don't get anxious because they're weak. They get anxious because they don't know what's going to happen.**

You walk into the exam room with a fuzzy sense that you "studied". You don't actually know what grade that studying bought you. Your brain fills the gap with the worst case. Heart rate up, hands cold, the first question looks like it's written in another language.

That is not a character flaw. It's what a brain does when it faces something important and uncertain. The fix is not to feel nothing. The fix is to remove the uncertainty, and then have a plan for the nerves that remain.

## Some anxiety is useful. Unprepared anxiety is the problem.

Let's be honest about this first, because a lot of "beat exam stress" advice pretends the goal is to feel calm and blissful. It isn't.

A bit of adrenaline sharpens you. It makes you read the question twice. It makes you check your working. Students who feel absolutely nothing walking into a three-hour paper often make careless errors because nothing in them is on alert.

The anxiety that wrecks exams is a different thing. It's the kind that comes from not knowing where you stand. It shows up as:

- Re-reading the same line of the question four times and taking nothing in
- A blank where a formula used to be
- Spending 20 minutes on question 1 because you're scared to move on
- Deciding halfway through that you've already failed, and coasting

Every one of those has the same root: **you don't have evidence that you're ready, so your brain assumes you're not.**

So the biggest lever isn't breathing exercises (though we'll get to those). It's evidence.

## The biggest lever: proof that you're ready

Here is the uncomfortable truth. Re-reading your notes cannot give you evidence of readiness. It gives you familiarity, which feels like readiness and isn't. We've written about why in [the testing effect post](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading), and it's the single most important idea on this page.

The only way to know what you'd get on the exam is to sit something like the exam and get it marked honestly.

That does three things for your nerves at once:

1. **It replaces "I think I'm okay" with a number.** A number can be worked on. A feeling can't.
2. **It makes the exam room familiar.** If you've sat five timed papers at your desk, the sixth one in a hall is just another paper.
3. **It shows you exactly which topics are the problem.** Anxiety loves vagueness. "I'm bad at chemistry" is terrifying. "I lose marks on equilibrium calculations" is a to-do list.

The trick is that the marking has to be honest. If you mark your own practice paper and give yourself half marks for "sort of getting it", you've just built false confidence, which is the thing that collapses in the exam room. Use the official marking scheme, or a tool that won't be kind to you. A wrong answer needs to score zero on the practice so it doesn't score zero on the real thing.

<Callout type="info">
The pattern is the same whether you're sitting NCEA, GCSE, HSC or QCE: the students who walk in calmest are usually the ones who already know roughly what mark they'd get, because they've measured it.
</Callout>

## The week before

By the week before, you should be done with new learning. If you're not, accept it, pick the two or three highest-value topics, and let the rest go. Trying to learn everything in the last week is how people arrive at the exam exhausted and convinced they know nothing.

What the week should look like:

**Sit at least two full practice papers under timed conditions.** Same time limit as the real thing. No phone. No notes. Mark them honestly. Write down every topic where you dropped marks.

**Spend the rest of the week on that list, and only that list.** [Retrieval practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testing_effect) on the weak topics. Not re-reading. Close the book, attempt a question, check, repeat. [Spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide) is your friend here: revisit a weak topic on day one, day three and day six rather than for three hours on day one.

**Fix your sleep now, not the night before.** If you've been going to bed at 1am, you can't suddenly sleep at 10pm on exam eve. Shift it earlier across the week.

**Do one thing each day that has nothing to do with the exam.** A surf, a run, a walk, a game with a mate. Not as a reward. As maintenance. A brain that has done nothing but revise for seven days walks into the exam already tired.

**Know the logistics.** Where is the exam? What time? What are you allowed to bring? What calculator? Sort this on Monday so it isn't a fresh worry on the day.

## The night before

The night before is not a study night. This is the hardest rule for anxious students to follow, because doing nothing feels dangerous. But the maths is simple: an hour of frantic revision at 11pm buys you almost nothing you'll retain, and costs you sleep you'll definitely miss.

Do this instead:

- **A light review, 30 to 45 minutes, of your own summary notes.** Formulas, key definitions, the structure of an essay. Skim, don't drill.
- **Pack your bag.** Pens, calculator, water, ID, whatever your exam requires. Physically put it by the door.
- **Write down tomorrow's plan on one line.** "Read the whole paper first. Start with the question I like most. Move on after the time's up." You'll re-read this in the morning.
- **Eat properly and stop screens an hour before bed.** Boring advice. Still true.
- **Go to bed at the time you've been practising all week.**

If you lie there and can't sleep, don't fight it. Resting in bed with your eyes shut still recovers a lot. Panicking about not sleeping is worse than not sleeping.

## The morning of

Get up with enough time that nothing is rushed. Rushing is a shortcut to adrenaline you don't need yet.

Eat something with actual substance. Not a sugar hit that crashes an hour into the paper.

Read your one-line plan.

Then, and this matters, **don't cram at the gate.** The huddle of students outside the hall testing each other on obscure facts is a machine for producing panic. Someone will ask about a topic you didn't cover. It'll rattle you. It won't be on the paper. Put your headphones in, or stand somewhere else.

If your hands are shaking as you walk in, notice it and name it: "that's adrenaline, it's supposed to be there." It's much less frightening once it has a name.

## The first five minutes in the room

This is where most exams are won or lost, and it has nothing to do with knowledge.

**Read the whole paper before you write anything.** Every question. Cover to cover. It takes three or four minutes and it does two crucial things: it tells your brain that the paper is finite and survivable, and it lets the harder questions start cooking in the background while you work on the easier ones.

**Work out your time per question** and write it in the margin. If the paper is 90 minutes and 6 questions, that's 15 minutes each. When you hit 15 minutes on a question, you move. You can come back.

**Start with the question you're most confident about.** Not question 1. The one you like. Getting marks on the board in the first ten minutes changes your whole state. Your brain goes from "we're in danger" to "we're working". Everything after that is easier.

**Read each question twice before answering.** Underline the command word: describe, explain, calculate, evaluate, compare. Half the marks lost in exams are lost to answering a different question from the one that was asked.

## What to do when you blank

It will happen at some point. You look at a question you know you can do and there's just nothing there. Here's the protocol.

1. **Don't stare at it.** Staring at a blank is how a two-minute blank becomes a ten-minute one. Put a small mark next to the question and move to the next one.
2. **Breathe once, slowly, out longer than in.** Not a whole meditation. One breath. Longer out than in is the bit that matters.
3. **Write anything related down.** A formula from the same topic. A definition. A diagram. Retrieval is a chain, and the blank usually clears once you've pulled on one nearby link.
4. **Come back to it after two other questions.** You'll be surprised how often the answer is just sitting there when you return.
5. **If it's still gone, write what you can and move on.** Partial working gets partial marks. A blank gets zero. Never leave a question empty if you can put down even one correct step.

Nobody has ever failed an exam because of one blank. People fail because one blank convinces them the whole paper is lost, and they stop trying on the questions they could have done.

<Callout type="warning">
The "I've already failed, so it doesn't matter" thought is the most expensive thought you can have in an exam. It's almost never true, and acting on it turns a rough paper into an actual fail. When you catch it, go to the next question you can do and do it.
</Callout>

## When it's more than exam nerves

Everything above is for the ordinary kind of anxiety, the kind that goes away when the exam does. Most people's does.

For some students it doesn't. If you're not sleeping for weeks, feeling sick most days, avoiding school, or the dread isn't tied to any particular exam, that's a different thing and it deserves a proper conversation with someone. A school counsellor is a good first stop. So is a GP. Talking to them is not an admission of anything. It's just sensible, the same way you'd get a sore knee looked at before a competition.

We're not going to pretend a blog post can fix that, and we'd rather say so than give you a breathing exercise and call it done.

## The short version

- Nerves are normal. Nerves without evidence of readiness are the problem.
- Get evidence: sit timed practice papers, marked honestly, and fix what they show you.
- The week before is for weak topics, sleep and one non-study thing a day.
- The night before is light review, pack the bag, and bed on time.
- Read the whole paper first. Start with what you like. Move on when the time's up.
- When you blank: mark it, breathe out, write something nearby, come back later.
- If it's bigger than exam nerves, talk to someone.
