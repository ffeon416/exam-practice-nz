**Most students who get Achieved in Level 2 English knew the text well enough for Merit.** They could retell the plot. They could name the techniques. They had a quote for every character. What they didn't have was an argument, so they wrote a summary with quotes in it and the marker gave them exactly what that's worth.

English is the subject where "studying" is hardest to define, because there's no set of methods to drill. But there is a set of skills, and they can be practised in a way that transfers to a silent room with a question you've never seen. This guide covers what the Level 2 externals test, what a Merit or Excellence response does that an Achieved one doesn't, how to build a text essay bank that survives an unseen question, how to read an unfamiliar text under pressure, and a timed-writing routine for the final four weeks.

## What the Level 2 English externals actually test

The externals sit in November, and which papers you sit depends on what your school has entered you for. Check with your teacher. Broadly, the externally assessed standards cover three things:

- **Written text.** An essay on a novel, play, short story collection or poetry you've studied. You choose one question from a list and argue a response using the text as evidence.
- **Visual or oral text.** The same essay task, but on a film, television, drama or other visual or oral text you've studied. You're expected to discuss the techniques of that medium (camera, sound, editing, staging) as well as ideas.
- **Unfamiliar texts.** Short passages you've never seen, usually a mix of prose, poetry and non-fiction. You're asked to explain how the writer uses language to create meaning and effect, with specific reference to the text in front of you.

At Level 2 the bar rises from Level 1 in one specific way: you're expected to go beyond describing what the writer does to analysing why it matters, and at the top grades to connect it to something beyond the text (the writer's purpose, the world the text comes from, the reader's own experience). The questions use words like "analyse", "convincingly" and "perceptively", and those words are the marking.

The other constant across all three papers is time. Every one of them is a writing test as much as a reading test. Students who know the text inside out and have never written a full essay in under an hour lose to students who know it adequately and have written twelve.

## What Merit and Excellence do that Achieved doesn't

The difference between the grades is not knowledge. It's what you do with it. The full breakdown, with the same answer shown at all three bands, is in [what examiners actually look for](/blog/achieved-merit-excellence-what-examiners-look-for), and the English-specific version is in [how to get Excellence in NCEA English](/blog/how-to-get-excellence-in-ncea-english). The short version:

| Band | What the response does | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Achieved | Identifies a technique or idea, gives a relevant example, explains what it shows | "Shakespeare uses a metaphor here to show Macbeth's ambition." |
| Merit | Does all of that, and analyses convincingly: explains how the technique creates the effect, links it back to the question, sustains the argument across the essay | "The metaphor of hidden fire suggests ambition Macbeth is ashamed of, which is why he asks the stars to conceal it rather than the audience." |
| Excellence | Does all of that, and is perceptive: connects the point to the writer's purpose, the wider world, or the reader, with insight that goes beyond the obvious reading | "...and Shakespeare lets us hear the wish before the crime, so that when the murder comes we already know it was chosen, not fated." |

Three habits separate the bands in practice.

**An argument, not a topic.** Achieved essays are about ambition in Macbeth. Excellence essays argue something about ambition in Macbeth that could be disagreed with. "Shakespeare shows ambition is dangerous" is a topic. "Shakespeare shows that ambition becomes dangerous at the moment it's hidden" is an argument.

**Effect, not just technique.** Naming the metaphor is Achieved. Explaining what it makes the reader feel or understand, and why the writer wanted that, is where Merit and Excellence live.

**A "so what" beyond the text.** At least once per paragraph at Excellence, the point reaches outside the page: to why the writer built it this way, to what it says about people, to how it lands on an audience now.

## How to prepare text essays for a question you haven't seen

The trap in text essays is preparing one essay and hoping the question fits. It rarely does, and forcing a prepared essay onto the wrong question is the most common way strong students drop to Achieved.

Prepare material, not essays.

### 1. Build a quote-and-technique bank

For each text, ten to fifteen quotes (or, for visual texts, moments: a shot, a cut, a sound cue) that you can reproduce exactly. For each one, write three things: the technique, the effect on the reader or viewer, and the idea it connects to. Keep it to a single page per text. If a quote doesn't do at least two jobs (works for two different ideas or two different characters), replace it.

Then learn the bank cold. Blank page, no notes, write it out. Repeat every few days. This is plain [retrieval practice](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading) and it's the one part of English that works exactly like maths: either you can produce the quote under pressure or you can't.

### 2. Write adaptable thesis lines

For each major idea in the text (ambition, guilt, power, isolation, whatever yours are), write one sentence that argues something about it. Not "the text explores guilt" but "the text shows guilt as the thing that punishes Macbeth when no one else can." Write four or five of these per text.

On the day, the question will ask about a character, an idea, a technique, a setting, or a relationship. One of your thesis lines will fit with a small rewrite. The paragraphs underneath come from the bank.

### 3. Practise to unseen questions, timed

This is the part most students skip. Get a list of past essay questions for your standard from NZQA's own published papers on the [NCEA site](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/), or have a teacher or a question generator produce new ones. Pick one you haven't seen, set the clock for the exam time, and write the full essay. Then mark it against the standard's language: did you analyse convincingly? Was there a perceptive point? Did every paragraph answer the question as asked?

Practising to a question you chose in advance is rehearsing a speech. Practising to an unseen one is rehearsing the exam.

<Callout type="info">
Plan for five minutes before you write. Thesis line, three paragraph points, one quote per point. A student with a five-minute plan and fifty minutes of writing beats a student who writes for fifty-five minutes and discovers their argument in paragraph three.
</Callout>

## How to approach unfamiliar texts

The unfamiliar-text paper punishes two habits: describing the text instead of analysing it, and answering a question you wish you'd been asked.

**Read the question before the text.** Every question tells you what to look for (how the writer creates a sense of place, how the writer builds tension, how the writer shows a character's attitude). Read the text with that lens, not blank.

**Annotate for technique and effect, together.** Underlining a metaphor is worth nothing on its own. Next to it, write what it does: "makes the house sound alive, uneasy." Five annotations with effects beat twenty underlinings.

**Quote short, then unpack.** A few words, quoted exactly, then two or three sentences on how those specific words work and why the writer chose them. Long quotes are a sign you're hoping the text will do the analysis for you.

**Answer the question asked.** If it asks how the writer creates tension, every sentence you write is about tension. Interesting observations about something else are marks you didn't earn.

**Structure by idea, not by line.** Walking through the text from top to bottom produces a summary. Grouping your points by what they do ("the writer builds tension through pace, then through what's left unsaid") produces analysis.

## Two worked examples

### Example A: one paragraph, from Achieved to Excellence

Question: **Analyse how the writer shows the danger of ambition.** Text: Macbeth.

**Achieved-level paragraph:**

> Shakespeare shows the danger of ambition through Macbeth. When Macbeth hears that Malcolm will be the next king, he says "Stars, hide your fires; Let not light see my black and deep desires." This is a metaphor. It shows that Macbeth has dark desires and wants to be king. This is dangerous because he will do anything to get there.

It has a point, a quote and an explanation. It's correct. It identifies a technique and says what it shows. And it doesn't argue anything a reader couldn't have guessed from the plot.

**Excellence-level rewrite:**

> Shakespeare shows that ambition becomes dangerous at the moment it has to be hidden. When Malcolm is named heir, Macbeth doesn't argue or plot aloud; he turns to the sky: "Stars, hide your fires; Let not light see my black and deep desires." The command to the stars is a metaphor for concealment, but what matters is who he's asking. He wants the light itself not to look, which tells us he already knows the desire is "black" and can't survive being seen. Shakespeare gives us the wish before the crime, in private, so that when the murder comes we know it was chosen, not fated. The danger of ambition, in this scene, isn't that Macbeth wants the crown. It's that he has learned to want it in the dark, and a man who can hide his intent from the heavens has nothing left to stop him.

**What changed.** The topic sentence became an argument (ambition is dangerous when hidden, not just dangerous). The quote is the same, but the analysis works on specific words ("black", "light", the command to the stars) rather than the metaphor as a label. The effect on the audience is explained (we hear the wish before the crime). And the last two sentences reach past the text to a claim about people, which is what "perceptive" means in the marking. Same knowledge. Different job done with it.

### Example B: an unfamiliar-text response, weak and then strong

The passage below is invented for this example, so you can see the method without a text you might know.

> *The house had been empty for a year, but it still breathed. Every board I stepped on answered. In the kitchen, the tap held a single drop and would not let it go.*

Question: **Explain how the writer creates an unsettling atmosphere.**

**Weak response:**

> The writer uses personification when they say the house "still breathed". This makes the house seem alive. They also use the words "empty" and "answered" to describe the house. The tap holding a drop is imagery. This all creates an unsettling atmosphere.

It names techniques and copies the question back at the end. It never explains why any of it is unsettling. The last sentence is an assertion, not analysis.

**Strong response:**

> The writer makes the house unsettling by giving it the small, involuntary behaviours of a living thing. "It still breathed" is personification, but the effect comes from "still": the house should have stopped, and hasn't, which makes the emptiness feel like a pause rather than an ending. The floorboards don't creak, they "answered", as if the narrator's footsteps are a question the house is waiting for. The final image narrows the tension to a single point: a tap that "held a single drop and would not let it go." The refusal is the unsettling part. Nothing here is threatening, but everything is withholding, and the reader is left waiting for something to be released.

**What changed.** Every technique is tied to a specific word and a specific effect. The response explains why the reader feels unsettled (the house is alive but withholding) instead of announcing that they do. And it's organised around one idea (involuntary life) rather than walking through the lines in order.

## Where the marks are lost

| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Retelling the plot | A paragraph that explains what happens before making a point | Start every paragraph with a claim; the text is evidence, not the story |
| Technique without effect | "This is a simile." Full stop. | Never name a technique without the next sentence saying what it does to the reader |
| Ignoring the question | A prepared essay on ambition when the question asked about a relationship | Rewrite your thesis line to the question's exact words in your plan |
| Long quotes | Five lines copied, one line of analysis | Quote a phrase, then spend three sentences on it |
| Running out of time | A strong first paragraph, a rushed second, no conclusion | Timed practice, every week, from now until the exam |
| No "so what" | Accurate analysis that never leaves the text | One sentence per paragraph on purpose, world or reader |

## The final 4 weeks: a timed-writing routine

You can't cram an essay subject, but you can rehearse one. This routine assumes three papers. Adjust if you're sitting fewer. Each session is 45 to 60 minutes.

| Week | Text essays | Unfamiliar texts | Bank and retrieval |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One full timed essay per text to an unseen question. Mark against the standard's language. List what was missing (argument? effect? "so what"?). | One timed unfamiliar-text response. Mark: did every point tie technique to effect? | Write out both quote banks from a blank page. Fill the gaps. |
| 2 | Two planned-only essays per text (5-minute plans to unseen questions, no writing). One full timed essay on your weaker text. | Two short passages, annotated for technique and effect only. One timed response. | Blank-page bank twice. Rewrite any thesis line that didn't fit last week's questions. |
| 3 | One full timed essay per text. Then rewrite the weakest paragraph from Achieved to Excellence, as in Example A. | Two timed responses on different text types (poetry, then non-fiction). | Blank-page bank once. Ten minutes of "which quote fits this question?" drills. |
| 4 | One timed essay per text early in the week. Read all your marked essays and the corrections. No new material after that. | One timed response early in the week. Then re-read your best one. | Blank-page bank on the day before each paper. Then stop. |

The pattern: every week you write under time, mark honestly, and fix one specific thing. By the exam you'll have written each essay six or more times to questions you didn't choose. That's the whole point. If you'd like the same structure across all your subjects, it lines up with the [8-week NCEA plan](/blog/how-to-study-for-ncea-exams).

<Callout type="warning">
Re-reading the novel or re-watching the film in the last fortnight feels like study and does almost nothing for your grade. You know the text. What you need is more reps of building an argument from it under time. If you catch yourself watching instead of writing, close the laptop and set a timer.
</Callout>

## Questions students ask

**How many quotes do I need per text?** Enough that three fit any likely question, plus spares. Ten to fifteen memorised exactly is plenty. More than that and you'll spend the exam choosing instead of writing.

**Can I use the same text for written text and visual text?** No. They're different standards, on different kinds of text. If you're sitting both, you need a bank for each.

**What if the question doesn't fit anything I've prepared?** It will fit something, once you rewrite your thesis line in the question's words. A question about a setting can be answered through a character's relationship to it. A question about a technique can be answered through the idea it serves. Spend your five planning minutes on the fit, then commit.

**How do I mark my own essay honestly?** Read it once for argument (could a reader disagree with your thesis?), once for effect (does every technique have a "so what"?), and once for the question (is every paragraph answering it?). Where any of those fail, that's your fix for next week. A teacher's mark is better if you can get one.
