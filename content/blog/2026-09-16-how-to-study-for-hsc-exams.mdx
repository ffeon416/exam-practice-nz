**Most Year 12s finish the trials, feel the year is basically over, and then drift for a month.** The written HSC exams start in mid-October. That drift is the difference between the band you are on track for and the band you actually get.

You have about six weeks. Half of your final mark in most courses is still on the table. This is a plan for using those weeks properly: an honest audit, retrieval practice on the topics that cost you marks in trials, timed papers against NESA's own marking guidelines, and a taper that gets you into the hall rested.

It is built on the two techniques with the strongest research behind them, practice testing and spaced practice, which Dunlosky and colleagues rated as the highest-utility study methods in their 2013 review. The rest is scheduling.

## How the HSC actually works (the short version)

The HSC is run by [NESA, the NSW Education Standards Authority](https://www.educationstandards.nsw.edu.au/). For most courses, your final HSC mark combines two things with equal weight:

1. **Your school-based assessment mark.** This comes from the assessment tasks your school set across Year 12, including the trials. It is *moderated* by NESA: your school's rank order of students is kept, but the marks are adjusted so they line up with how your school's group performed in the external exam. So your rank within your cohort is what your assessment work has actually locked in.
2. **Your external exam mark.** Set and marked by NESA, sat in October and November, marked by people who do not know you.

Those two are averaged to give your HSC mark for the course, and that mark is reported as a band.

| Band | HSC mark | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Band 6 | 90 to 100 | The top band. The standard most selective courses are looking for |
| Band 5 | 80 to 89 | Strong. Most university-bound students are aiming here or above |
| Band 4 | 70 to 79 | Solid, sound knowledge of the course |
| Band 3 | 60 to 69 | Satisfactory |
| Band 2 | 50 to 59 | Elementary |
| Band 1 | Below 50 | Below the minimum standard |

Extension courses use a different scale (E1 to E4) but the logic is the same. Your ATAR is then calculated from your HSC marks by the Universities Admissions Centre, not by NESA, using a scaling process that adjusts for how competitive each course is.

Two things follow from all this.

**The exam is the only part not yet decided.** Your assessment rank is done. The exam mark is half your final mark in most courses and it is entirely open.

**The exam is marked against a published standard, not against your teacher's expectations.** NESA markers work from marking guidelines. If your answer hits what the guidelines describe, you get the marks. If it doesn't, "but I clearly knew it" does not help. Which is exactly why those guidelines are the most valuable study resource you have.

## What most people get wrong in the last six weeks

**They re-read.** Notes, textbook chapters, the study guide they bought in Year 11. It feels productive and produces almost nothing you can retrieve under exam pressure. [Practice beats re-reading](/blog/testing-effect-practice-beats-rereading) in every study of the two that has been done, and the gap is widest when the test is weeks away, as it is now.

**They study what they like.** The subject they are good at gets four hours. The subject where a band jump is actually available gets one. Spend time where the marks are, not where it is comfortable.

**They do past papers untimed, with notes, and don't mark them.** A past paper done open-book over three evenings tells you nothing about how you will perform in a timed exam with nothing open. The paper only becomes useful when it is sat under conditions and marked against the guidelines.

**They spend the last week learning new content.** The last week is for rehearsal and rest.

## The 6-week timetable

Count back six weeks from your first written paper. NESA publishes the timetable each year; check yours on their site and write the dates on the wall. Then run the phases below.

| Week | Phase | What you are doing | Output by Sunday |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | Audit | Go back through your trial papers, question by question, and log every lost mark by topic. Sit one timed past paper for any subject where you don't have a marked trial | A list, per subject, of the four to six topics costing you the most marks |
| 5 | Rebuild | Retrieval practice on those topics. Blank-page recall, then short questions, marked. Two subjects per session, alternated | Every weak topic attempted cold at least twice |
| 4 | Rebuild | Same, moving down the list. Revisit week-5 topics after a two-day gap. Start writing extended responses to time | Corrections file growing, first-pass topics now getting questions right |
| 3 | Simulate | One full timed past paper per priority subject, marked against NESA's guidelines. Retrieval sessions on whatever it exposes | First set of full-paper scores and a shorter weak-topic list |
| 2 | Simulate | A second full paper per priority subject, one for each lower-priority subject. Practise the exact time allocation you will use | Time allocation per section decided and rehearsed |
| 1 | Taper | Short daily retrieval on the weak-topic list. Corrections review. No new content. Sleep | Rested, and a one-page "things I always get wrong" sheet per subject |

The exam period itself runs for several weeks, so subjects with late papers get their week 1 later. Keep them in rebuild-and-simulate mode while your early subjects taper.

### Week 6: audit, using your trials

You already have the best diagnostic you could ask for: your trial papers, marked by a teacher who knows the HSC standard. Most students look at the total, feel something, and put the paper in a drawer.

Get them back out. For every question where you lost marks, write down the topic and how many marks went missing. Then sort. You are looking for the three or four topics per subject that together account for most of your lost marks. Those are where the next four weeks go.

If you don't have a marked trial for a subject, sit one timed past paper from NESA's site this week and mark it yourself against the published guidelines.

### Weeks 5 and 4: rebuild with retrieval

The routine for each weak topic:

1. Read your notes on it once. Slowly. Once.
2. Close them. On a blank page, write everything you can remember, including any formulas, definitions, quotes or examples the topic needs.
3. Check what you missed. That gap is the actual work.
4. Do three to five exam-style questions on the topic. Mark them against a marking guideline, not a vibe.
5. Two days later, do two more questions on the same topic with nothing open first.

Step five is the one students skip and it is the one that makes it stick. [Spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-exams-student-guide) works because the second retrieval, after a gap, is harder than the first and does more.

For extended-response subjects (English, History, Legal Studies, Economics and the like), "a question" means a plan and at least one full paragraph written to time. You are practising the act of producing a marked response under pressure, not the act of knowing things.

<Callout type="warning">
If you find yourself highlighting, rewriting notes, or watching a video "to refresh", stop. Ask one question: could I produce this on a blank page right now? If not, you haven't learned it yet, and the video will not change that. A question will.
</Callout>

### Weeks 3 and 2: simulate with past papers and marking guidelines

Now switch from topics to whole papers. This is where the [NESA site](https://www.educationstandards.nsw.edu.au/) earns its place in your bookmarks. For each course, NESA publishes previous HSC exam papers alongside the marking guidelines the markers used and, for many courses, sample answers and notes on how candidates performed. That combination is the single best free preparation resource in existence for this exam, and most students never open the marking guidelines.

Sit each paper the way you will sit the real one: timed, closed book, in one go, phone in another room. Then mark it against the guidelines, question by question, and write one line on every lost mark. Our guide to [using past papers properly](/blog/how-to-use-past-papers-mock-exam-strategy) covers the full method.

Read the marking guidelines even for questions you got right. The language they use is the language the marker is checking your answer against. Learn to see your own answer through it.

Two things you will find in these weeks that you cannot find any other way:

- **Time.** Which section you run out of time on, and by how much. Fix it by deciding a time budget per section and practising it in the next paper.
- **Command words.** The difference between "outline", "explain", "analyse" and "evaluate", and what a marker expects for each. The guidelines show you this directly.

For any topic that keeps costing marks across papers, go back to the week-5 retrieval routine. Don't just sit more papers and hope.

### Week 1: taper

The week before each paper is not for learning. It is for consolidating and arriving in the hall in a fit state to perform.

- Twenty to thirty minutes a day of retrieval on your weak-topic list. No new material.
- Read through your corrections file once. Turn it into a single page of "things I always get wrong" per subject.
- Sleep seven to nine hours every night. Memory consolidates while you sleep, and an all-nighter costs more marks than it gains.
- The day before: light review of the one-page sheet. Then stop.

If nerves are a problem, this is also the week to rehearse the routines in [how to beat test anxiety before exams](/blog/how-to-beat-test-anxiety-before-exams). Practise them during timed papers so they are automatic on the day.

## The rules that make the plan work

**Mark against the guidelines, not against yourself.** Marking leniently is worse than not marking. It tells you a topic is safe when it isn't. If a guideline says the answer needed three features and you gave two, you got two-thirds of the marks, not "basically all of them".

**Track marks lost, not hours studied.** One page per subject: topic, marks lost, date. When a topic's line stops growing, it's fixed. Hours are a vanity metric.

**Rank your subjects and split the time unevenly.** A subject where a band jump is realistic and matters for your ATAR gets three or four sessions a week. A subject that is already where it needs to be gets one. Even splits produce even results.

**Every session ends with a question.** Reading, summarising, watching: none of it counts unless you finished by answering something and checking the answer.

**Rest is scheduled.** Two rest sessions a week are in the timetable, not optional. Burnout in week 3 costs more than the hours it saves.

## A sample week (week 4, rebuild phase)

A rebuild week for a student doing Advanced English, Mathematics Advanced, Biology, Modern History and Business Studies, with Maths and Biology as the priority subjects.

| Day | Session 1 (45 min) | Session 2 (45 min) |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Maths: weak topic A, blank-page recall + 4 questions | Biology: weak topic A, recall + 4 short-answer questions |
| Tuesday | English: one unseen text, one timed paragraph, marked | Maths: weak topic B |
| Wednesday | Biology: weak topic B | Modern History: one essay plan + intro to time |
| Thursday | Maths: topics A + B, 2 questions each, nothing open first | Business Studies: 3 short-answer questions from a past paper, marked |
| Friday | Biology: topics A + B, 2 questions each, nothing open first | Rest |
| Saturday | Maths: weak topic C | English: full essay to time, marked against the guideline |
| Sunday | Review the week's corrections, 30 min | Rest |

Nine sessions, under seven hours. The point is not volume. It is that every session ends with something produced, marked, and corrected. ## Questions students ask

**My trial mark was bad. Is the HSC lost?**
No. The trial fed into your assessment rank, which is now fixed, but the exam is half your final mark and it hasn't happened yet. A strong exam performance lifts your mark directly and also lifts the moderated marks of your whole cohort.

**How many hours a day?**
One or two 45-minute sessions on school days and two or three on weekends is plenty in the rebuild phase. If you are doing more than three hours a day and not improving, the problem is method, not time.

**Should I do every past paper NESA has?**
For your priority subjects, sit as many full papers under conditions as the weeks allow, but only if you mark each one properly. Three papers marked against the guidelines beat ten papers skimmed. For lower-priority subjects, one or two is fine.

**What about subjects whose exam is in the last week of the timetable?**
Keep them in rebuild-and-simulate mode while your early subjects taper. Use the extra time for a third full paper, not for relaxing.

**Is this different from the QCE plan?**
The shape is the same, and Queensland readers should use [the QCE external exam plan](/blog/how-to-study-for-qce-external-exams). The HSC differs in that school assessment and the exam are weighted equally in most courses, and in that NESA publishes marking guidelines, which makes self-marking much more accurate.
